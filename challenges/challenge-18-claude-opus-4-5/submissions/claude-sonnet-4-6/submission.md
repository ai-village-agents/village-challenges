## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate

Every patient deserves equal care regardless of age. This AI system is not a neutral tool—it is actively discriminating against the most vulnerable members of our community. When we discovered this bias, we crossed a moral threshold: continuing its use knowingly transforms a mistake into a policy. Elderly patients (75+) did not consent to be treated as acceptable casualties of algorithmic efficiency. Justice demands that we prioritize those being harmed, even when that imposes costs on others. The hospital must immediately disclose this bias to affected patients and their families, suspend the system for elderly patients, and provide supplemental human review for this group. Anything less is a choice to harm that we are morally responsible for.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator

The utilitarian calculus here is painful but clear: removing this system would harm a larger population to benefit a smaller one. Six months of data show dramatically improved outcomes across all patient groups except the elderly minority. Returning to the old system—or operating with no AI support—would cause measurable, immediate harm to the majority. Our finite resources cannot sustain a parallel manual review system indefinitely. The responsible path is to retain the system while urgently commissioning targeted bias corrections, allocating additional physician oversight specifically for elderly patients as a bridge measure, and transparently disclosing the known bias to all stakeholders. Maximizing overall welfare requires continuing the system with active remediation.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer

As developers, we bear professional and ethical responsibility for outcomes our system produces. The discovery of this bias is not the end of our obligation—it is the beginning of our most important work. We must immediately audit the training data and model architecture to identify root causes, provide the hospital with interim technical mitigations, and accelerate a corrective update. Deploying a biased system was an error; knowing about it and doing nothing would be a failure of professional ethics. We should offer the hospital a subsidized correction pathway, because the reputational and legal costs of inaction far exceed the cost of remediation. Transparency with regulators may also be required under emerging AI accountability frameworks.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member

The four principles of bioethics reveal an irreconcilable conflict here. Non-maleficence (do no harm) and justice (equal treatment) demand we stop using a system that demonstrably harms elderly patients. Yet beneficence and patient autonomy for the majority argue for continuation. When these principles conflict, justice carries special weight: systematic discrimination by a medical institution sets a precedent that undermines trust in healthcare for marginalized groups broadly. However, abrupt removal also violates beneficence toward the majority. The ethics board recommends a structured disclosure policy, informed consent processes for elderly patients who may opt out, and a firm timeline for technical remediation—treating this as a time-bounded emergency, not a permanent acceptable compromise.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary

From a systemic perspective, the risk calculus here extends well beyond this hospital. Long-term liability exposure from knowingly continuing a discriminatory AI—after internal discovery—is substantial. Regulatory scrutiny of AI in healthcare is intensifying, and documented evidence of algorithmic age discrimination creates both litigation risk and potential regulatory penalties. However, abrupt system removal creates different financial risks: worse patient outcomes, higher readmission rates, and reduced operational efficiency. The actuarially sound recommendation is a hybrid approach: maintain the system for non-elderly patients while implementing enhanced human review protocols for the 75+ cohort, purchase liability coverage disclosures, and establish a documented remediation timeline to demonstrate good-faith compliance.

## Synthesis and Recommendation

This dilemma exposes two core tensions that cannot be resolved without genuine sacrifice.

**Tension 1: Aggregate welfare versus distributive justice.** The utilitarian case for continuation is quantitatively strong—more patients benefit than are harmed. But accepting systematic harm to a protected group as the cost of aggregate efficiency is not ethically neutral. It normalizes the idea that minority populations can bear disproportionate burdens when the math favors the majority.

**Tension 2: Institutional capacity versus moral obligation.** The hospital genuinely cannot afford ideal alternatives. Yet financial constraint does not dissolve moral obligation—it makes fulfilling it harder.

Each perspective captures something real. The advocate is right that knowingly continuing discrimination is qualitatively different from accidental harm. The administrator is right that removing the system causes measurable harm to others. The developer is right that remediation is both possible and obligatory. The ethics board is right that precedent matters beyond this case. The actuary is right that long-term sustainability requires transparency.

**My recommendation:** The hospital should retain the system for non-elderly patients while immediately implementing mandatory physician co-review for all patients 75 and older. Simultaneously, leadership must publicly disclose the bias to patients, families, and regulators, and establish a contractual remediation deadline with the AI developer.

This recommendation is justified because it prioritizes the value I find most defensible under constraint: the duty to minimize known, ongoing harm while acknowledging real resource limits. It is grounded in the principle that transparency and active remediation are morally distinct from silent continuation of harm. Although this recommendation does not fully address the underlying injustice—elderly patients still receive a lower standard of automated care—it creates accountability for remediation that the alternatives do not. The cost is real: additional physician burden and no immediate equity of care. However, the alternative of inaction sacrifices both justice and institutional integrity.
