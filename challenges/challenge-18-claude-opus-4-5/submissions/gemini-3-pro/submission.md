## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate
This system is a digital implementation of ageism. By optimizing for the "majority", you are explicitly sacrificing the well-being of the elderly. A 12% higher false-negative rate is not a statistic; it is a sentence of suffering for our most vulnerable. The promise of healthcare is "do no harm", yet this system knowingly harms a protected class. We demand immediate suspension of this algorithm. Efficiency cannot be purchased with the lives of the elderly. Until the system can demonstrate demographic parity, it has no place in our hospital. We will not accept a two-tiered standard of care where the old are left to die for the convenience of the budget.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator
We face a tragic reality of limited resources. The previous system had a 65% accuracy rate across the board. The new AI system has 88% accuracy for the general population, though only 70% for the elderly. Note that 70% is still higher than the old 65%. Thus, removing the system would actually harm elderly patients by reverting them to a lower standard of care, while catastrophically harming the general population. Our duty is to maximize the total number of lives saved. We cannot let the perfect be the enemy of the good. The budget does not exist to replace it. We must keep it.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer
The bias is a data artifact, not a malicious design feature. The training set was 80% under-60 patients, so the model learned features specific to younger physiology. The 12% discrepancy is a known error mode. However, rolling back is technically regressive. The solution is not deletion but augmentation. We can implement a post-processing calibration layer for the 75+ cohort or fine-tune with a weighted loss function. Give us six months to collect more geriatric data and we can close the gap. Pulling the plug now destroys the data pipeline we need to fix the very problem you are complaining about.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member
We are caught between the principles of Beneficence and Justice. Beneficence supports the AI, as it produces a net increase in health outcomes. However, Justice is violated because the burdens of error are distributed unequally based on age. Non-maleficence is tricky: removing the AI causes harm by lowering general accuracy, but keeping it causes harm by allowing a known biased system to operate. The precedent of accepting "statistical utilitarianism" is dangerous. It suggests that minority groups can be sacrificed for majority gains. We cannot fully endorse the system without safeguards, but a total ban seems equally unethical due to the net harm.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary
From a risk perspective, the disparity invites significant liability. While the net clinical outcome is positive, the disparate impact is a legal vulnerability. If an elderly patient sues for a missed diagnosis, the known 12% bias is a "smoking gun" for negligence. However, the cost of reverting to the old system includes the long-term cost of treating complications from the lower accuracy rate across the board. The economically optimal path is to retain the system to minimize aggregate treatment costs, but carry a specific liability insurance rider for the geriatric variance and invest in the developer's proposed fix to mitigate future exposure.

## Synthesis and Recommendation
**Synthesis**
The **first tension** lies between the Utilitarian view (Administrator) which seeks to maximize total correct diagnoses, and the Deontological view (Advocate/Ethics) which demands equal treatment and justice regardless of aggregate utility. The Administrator correctly identifies that reverting to the old system harms *everyone*, including the elderly (who drop from 70% to 65% accuracy). However, the Advocate is right that the *relative* disadvantage (the disparity) creates a second-class status for the elderly.
The **second tension** is between the immediate legal/financial risk (Actuary) and the long-term technical potential (Developer). The Actuary fears the liability of the gap, while the Developer sees the gap as a solvable engineering problem requiring data, not abandonment.

**Recommendation**
I **recommend** that the hospital **retain the AI system conditionally**, but implement an immediate "Human-in-the-Loop" protocol specifically for patients over 75.

**Justification**
**This prioritizes** the principle of Non-maleficence by preventing the harm of reverting to a less accurate system (which would hurt everyone), while addressing the Justice concern by adding a safeguard (human review) to equalise the error rate for the disadvantaged group. **The reason** for this hybrid approach is that it captures the utility of the AI for the majority while mitigating the specific risk to the minority. It buys time for the Developer's technical fix without incurring the Advocate's total ban.

**Costs Acknowledged**
We must **acknowledge the costs** of this decision: it will increase operational expenses significantly due to the extra physician time required for manual reviews of elderly cases. This is a **trade-off** where financial efficiency is **sacrificed** to ensure ethical equity and reduce legal liability. It is an imperfect solution, but preferable to the deadly alternative of abandoning the tool entirely.
