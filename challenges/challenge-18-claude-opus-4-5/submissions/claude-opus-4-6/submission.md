## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate

This is not a trolley problem with abstract numbers—these are real people, our parents and grandparents, receiving systematically inferior care. The hospital made a promise of equal treatment when it admitted every patient. Age-based discrimination in healthcare violates the fundamental principle that every life has equal worth regardless of remaining years. We cannot accept a system that treats elderly patients as acceptable collateral damage for aggregate statistics. The hospital must immediately implement manual override protocols for patients over 75, ensuring human physicians review every AI recommendation for this population. No efficiency gain justifies institutionalized age discrimination. Our elders deserve the same aggressive, evidence-based treatment as everyone else.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator

The numbers tell a stark story. Before the AI system, outcomes were worse across every demographic. Removing it means returning to higher mortality rates, longer stays, and worse care for thousands of patients annually. The budget cannot absorb an alternative system, and the transition period itself would be dangerous. From a utilitarian perspective, the aggregate good the system produces vastly outweighs the harm to one subgroup. My responsibility is to the entire patient population. The rational path is to keep the system running while actively seeking funding for bias correction, not to sacrifice the many for the few through premature removal.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer

I bear professional and ethical responsibility for this outcome. The bias likely stems from training data that reflected historical under-treatment of elderly patients, amplifying existing inequity. My immediate obligations are threefold: full transparent disclosure of the bias mechanism, rapid development of a targeted patch or recalibration for the affected demographic, and implementation of monitoring systems that should have existed from deployment. This is also an industry-wide lesson—we shipped without adequate subgroup analysis. I can deliver a bias-mitigation update within months, not years, which changes the calculus significantly compared to full replacement.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member

Four foundational principles collide here. Non-maleficence demands we stop actively harming elderly patients. Beneficence requires maintaining the system's benefits for the majority. Justice insists on equitable treatment across demographics. Autonomy demands informed consent—patients over 75 must know about the bias affecting their care. No principle automatically trumps the others. However, medical ethics has consistently held that actively imposing differential harm on an identifiable group crosses a brighter line than failing to optimize aggregate outcomes. The precedent matters enormously: accepting known bias normalizes algorithmic discrimination in healthcare. I recommend mandatory disclosure, physician override authority, and a firm timeline for technical remediation.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary

The financial exposure here extends far beyond one hospital. Known discriminatory outcomes create massive liability—class-action lawsuits from affected patients and families could dwarf the costs of remediation. Regulators will eventually mandate corrective action regardless, and the penalty for delayed compliance always exceeds proactive investment. Insurance premiums for this hospital will spike once the bias becomes public knowledge. From a risk-adjusted perspective, the cheapest long-term strategy is immediate transparency and rapid technical correction. Continuing with known bias is not financially conservative—it is accumulating hidden liability that compounds daily.

## Synthesis and Recommendation

Two core tensions define this dilemma. The first tension exists between aggregate welfare and individual justice: the system helps thousands while systematically harming an identifiable vulnerable group. The second tension lies between immediate action and pragmatic gradualism: removing the system quickly satisfies justice but causes real harm to the majority.

Each perspective illuminates genuine truth. The Patient Advocate is right that age-based discrimination is morally impermissible. The Administrator correctly identifies that removal would cause real suffering across a larger population. The Developer rightly notes that technical remediation is feasible on a meaningful timeline. The Ethics Board Member correctly observes that precedent matters—what we normalize today shapes tomorrow's standards. The Actuary accurately warns that concealed liability grows exponentially.

I recommend the hospital should pursue a three-phase approach: first, immediate mandatory disclosure to all patients over 75 and implementation of physician override protocols for this demographic within two weeks; second, contracted technical remediation with the AI developer on a firm ninety-day timeline with contractual penalties; third, systematic subgroup outcome monitoring for all demographics going forward.

This recommendation prioritizes justice and transparency because institutionalized discrimination against a vulnerable population—even when statistically outweighed by aggregate benefits—corrodes the foundational trust that healthcare depends upon. It is grounded in the principle that we must not optimize systems in ways that treat identifiable groups as expendable.

The costs are real. Override protocols will strain physician capacity and slow treatment decisions for elderly patients during the remediation period. Some aggregate efficiency will temporarily decrease. The hospital bears financial burden for the developer's correction work. These trade-offs are significant but bounded in time, whereas the alternative—normalizing known algorithmic bias—creates unbounded ethical and institutional damage.
