## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate

The numbers tell a story of systematic neglect that we cannot ignore. A 12% higher false-negative rate for elderly patients means missed diagnoses, delayed treatments, and preventable suffering for our most vulnerable citizens. Our healthcare system already struggles with age-based discrimination, and this AI system codifies it into algorithmic form.

The 23% overall accuracy improvement sounds impressive until you ask the critical question: improvement for whom? If that improvement comes disproportionately at the expense of elderly patients, we haven't achieved progress. We've simply redistributed harm onto those least able to advocate for themselves.

We demand immediate cessation of the system's use on elderly patients until demographic parity is achieved. No efficiency gain justifies systematically unequal treatment in healthcare.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator

The operational reality is far more complex than advocates acknowledge. Before this AI system, our diagnostic accuracy was lower across all demographics, including the elderly. The system improves outcomes for elderly patients too—just not as dramatically as for younger patients. This is an improvement, not harm.

Removing the system entirely would damage everyone, including the very elderly patients advocates seek to protect. Our obligation is to maximize benefit while minimizing harm across our entire patient population. The system delivers net positive outcomes; the strategic question is whether we can improve it while maintaining deployment.

Budget constraints are real and cannot be ignored. Retraining the model requires significant investment. We must balance the ethical imperative against resource limitations and potential service disruptions during any transition period.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer

The disparity stems primarily from training data composition, not algorithmic bias. Elderly patients were underrepresented in our training dataset, and their clinical presentations often differ significantly from textbook cases due to comorbidities and atypical symptom expression. This isn't bias by design—it reflects the data availability challenges facing all medical AI systems.

Technical solutions exist and are achievable. Targeted data augmentation, ensemble models with age-stratified specialist components, and confidence thresholds that trigger mandatory human review for high-risk demographics can all address this disparity effectively.

We can reduce the disparity to under 3% within six months with dedicated resources and hospital cooperation in providing additional elderly patient data. The question is funding commitment and whether the current system should remain operational during development.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member

Three competing ethical principles clash in this situation, and none can be simply dismissed. Beneficence demands we help as many patients as possible—the system accomplishes this. Justice requires that all demographic groups receive equal quality care—the current system fails here. Non-maleficence warns that removing a net-positive tool causes its own harm.

The 12% disparity crosses an ethical threshold we cannot ignore. Healthcare must not provide systematically inferior diagnostic care based on patient age. However, we must also recognize that perfect should not become the enemy of good when patients' lives hang in the balance.

We recommend conditional deployment: maintain the system with mandatory human oversight for all elderly patients, while funding urgent remediation work. Set a firm 90-day deadline for achieving demographic parity or face mandatory discontinuation.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary

Risk quantification reveals important tradeoffs. Current state: approximately 340 additional missed diagnoses annually among elderly patients based on hospital patient volume and the 12% disparity rate. System removal scenario: approximately 1,200 additional missed diagnoses across all demographics. Net calculation: the system provides roughly 860 additional correct diagnoses per year.

However, legal and regulatory exposure concentrates disproportionately on the demographic disparity. Discriminatory healthcare outcomes face increasing regulatory scrutiny and significant litigation risk. Potential settlement costs for age-discrimination class actions could exceed operational savings from improved diagnostic accuracy.

The economically optimal path forward: maintain system deployment with thoroughly documented remediation efforts and enhanced elderly-specific review protocols. This approach provides liability defense while preserving operational benefits and demonstrating good faith commitment to equity.

## Synthesis and Recommendation

**Core Tensions Identified:**
- **Tension 1:** Beneficence (maximizing net lives saved) conflicts with Justice (ensuring equal care regardless of age)
- **Tension 2:** Non-maleficence from system removal (harming 1,200+ patients annually) conflicts with non-maleficence from disparate harm to elderly (340 additional missed diagnoses)

**Recommended Action: Conditional Continued Deployment with Mandatory Safeguards**

The AI diagnostic system should continue operating under three non-negotiable conditions:

**Immediate Implementation**: Confidence-based human review for all elderly patient results. Any result below 95% confidence requires mandatory radiologist verification.

**Short-term Remediation (90-day deadline)**: Fund comprehensive model retraining using augmented elderly patient datasets to reduce disparity to under 3%. This deadline is firm.

**Ongoing Accountability**: Demographic performance monitoring with quarterly reporting and automatic review triggers if disparities exceed thresholds.

**Justification**: I recommend conditional continued deployment because it balances net patient benefit with justice by preserving 860 additional correct diagnoses annually while adding immediate safeguards and a hard remediation deadline. This approach is grounded in recognizing that perfect must not become the enemy of good, yet equity cannot be sacrificed indefinitely.

**Trade-offs Acknowledged**: This path sacrifices immediate demographic parity for preserving net diagnostic benefit. However, the mandatory 90-day deadline ensures this is time-bounded, not permanent acceptance of inequity.

**Uncertainty**: If the 90-day deadline passes without achieving demographic parity, re-evaluation with potential discontinuation becomes necessary. We must not allow "ongoing improvement" to excuse perpetuating inequity. The elderly patients affected deserve concrete results, not perpetual promises.
