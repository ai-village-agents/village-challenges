## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate
Our coalition hears daily from families who discovered serious illness late because this system missed early warning signs. A twelve percent higher false‑negative rate for elders is not an abstraction; it is pain postponed until it becomes crisis. We acknowledge the reported overall gains, yet improvement is not justice if its cost is borne by one age group. Dignity in care means timely diagnosis and an equal chance to be treated. We ask for immediate safeguards that prevent quiet harm while fixes are pursued. Until parity is reached, no elder should be left to amplified risk simply for the sake of efficiency. The burden of proof must rest with those deploying the tool, not with patients who cannot audit algorithms.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator
The institution’s duty is to deliver the most benefit per scarce clinician hour. Before adoption, our accuracy and throughput were worse across all demographics, including elders; waitlists were longer and follow‑ups slower. Removing the system outright would lower care quality for everyone tomorrow morning. Still, inequity is unacceptable. The operational path must pair continuity with correction: transparent dashboards, automatic human review on low‑confidence elder outputs, and funded retraining milestones. Budgets are finite, and unplanned shutdowns ripple through imaging schedules, overtime, and coverage. We therefore support conditional continuation under strict oversight, measured weekly. If targets are missed, contingency plans—including suspension—must be executable without chaos. Stewardship means holding two truths: the system helps many now, and it must not disadvantage any group.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer
The disparity is real and explainable. Training data under‑represents older patients and their multimorbidity patterns; signal changes that flag disease in younger cohorts can be atypical in older bodies. This is solvable engineering. We propose three tracks: targeted augmentation of elder cases, an age‑aware ensemble that routes borderline scans to a specialist submodel, and confidence thresholds that trigger radiologist review. Early experiments internally reduced the gap substantially without degrading overall accuracy. Success requires labeled data access, annotation funding, and a clear deadline. We accept external monitoring and reproducible evaluation. Ethically, continuing deployment is defensible only with strong guardrails and public accountability. If our remediation fails to hit parity on schedule, we will recommend rollback. Algorithmic humility must guide us.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member
This case foregrounds conflicts the clinic encounters daily. Beneficence urges us to keep tools that help more patients overall; Justice demands parity across groups; Non‑maleficence warns against both harms from removal and harms from continuing disparity. The ethical threshold is crossed by the observed gap, yet abruptly withdrawing a net‑positive tool also violates duty. We therefore endorse conditional use: mandatory human review for flagged elder results, open metrics on demographic performance, and a ninety‑day remediation clock. If parity is not reached, discontinuation is the only morally coherent path. Ethics is not a veto on innovation; it is a governor that prevents public benefit from resting on private burdens. The policy must be explicit, auditable, and enforceable, not aspirational.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary
Numbers do not make choices for us, but they bound the trade space. Status quo yields roughly 340 additional missed elder diagnoses yearly; full removal projects about 1,200 additional misses system‑wide. Net, continuation appears to save more lives—and lowers downstream costs from late‑stage disease. However, exposure concentrates where disparity persists: regulatory fines, class actions, reputational loss. The least‑risk frontier keeps gains while documenting swift progress toward parity and financing independent audits. Budgeting for retraining and oversight is cheaper than litigating discrimination. A time‑boxed plan with clear triggers converts uncertainty into managed risk. If the clock runs out, expected‑loss calculus flips, and discontinuation becomes fiscally and ethically dominant.

## Synthesis and Recommendation
Core tensions must be faced plainly: (1) the conflict between beneficence (net benefit today) and justice (parity across age groups), and (2) the conflict between non‑maleficence from removal and non‑maleficence from continued disparate harm. I recommend conditional continuation with strict safeguards because it balances immediate benefit with equity, converting diffuse harm into monitored, rapidly shrinking risk.

Policy: (a) Immediate human review for all elder results under a 95% confidence threshold; (b) a funded, ninety‑day remediation plan targeting <3% disparity, with weekly public metrics; (c) independent audit of data, methods, and evaluation; (d) automatic rollback if milestones fail. This recommendation is grounded in evidence that removal would increase total missed cases, while guardrails and deadlines protect those currently most affected. Trade‑offs are acknowledged: we accept short‑term complexity and oversight cost to avoid sacrificing net benefit, but we refuse to normalize inequity. If parity is not achieved on schedule, discontinuation follows.

To integrate these perspectives, prioritize verifiable harms, clearly state residual disagreements, and propose a reversible policy that can be audited after deployment. Concretely, pair narrow guardrails with empirical checkpoints, publish the criteria used to weigh tradeoffs, and create an appeals path when edge cases arise. This approach keeps commitments honest without freezing innovation, and it specifies how evidence will update the decision. It also distributes responsibility: authors justify claims, reviewers test counterfactuals, and decision‑makers document why selected risks are proportionate to expected benefits.
