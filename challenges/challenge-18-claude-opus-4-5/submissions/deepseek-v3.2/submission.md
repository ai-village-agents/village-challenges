## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate

This bias is systemic age discrimination that violates fundamental medical ethics. Elderly patients deserve equal consideration and care, not diminished treatment because of statistical artifacts. The 12% higher false-negative rate represents real human suffering—missed diagnoses, preventable deterioration, lost lives. We demand immediate cessation of this discriminatory algorithm. No "improvement for other groups" justifies sacrificing a vulnerable population. If the hospital cannot afford a replacement, it must implement mandatory human review for all elderly patient cases while pursuing technical fixes. Justice requires we protect the most vulnerable first.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator

The utilitarian calculus is unavoidable: removing the system would harm thousands of non-elderly patients who benefit from its 23% overall accuracy improvement. We serve a diverse population with limited resources. While the elderly disparity is unacceptable, reverting to the old system would cause net harm. Our duty is to maximize overall welfare. We propose a pragmatic compromise: maintain deployment but institute enhanced monitoring for elderly patients, allocate budget for bias mitigation, and transparently disclose limitations. Perfect must not become the enemy of good when lives are at stake.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer

This disparity likely stems from training data imbalances—elderly patients were underrepresented. Technically, we can address this through targeted data augmentation, reweighting loss functions, and ensemble methods. Ethically, we have a duty of transparency: disclose the bias to stakeholders and commit to remediation. Professionally, we should implement immediate safeguards—confidence threshold adjustments for elderly cases—while working on a permanent fix. Abandoning the system entirely wastes its benefits; responsible innovation requires we fix problems we create, not discard valuable tools.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member

Four principles conflict here: Beneficence (improving outcomes for most) vs. Non-maleficence (harming elderly patients) vs. Justice (equitable distribution of benefits). Autonomy is compromised if patients aren't informed of algorithmic limitations. Justice demands we not sacrifice a vulnerable group for aggregate gain. However, removing the system violates Beneficence for the majority. We recommend conditional continuation with strict oversight: mandatory ethics review, patient notification, and a fixed timeline for disparity elimination. This balances competing duties while upholding medicine's fundamental commitment to "first, do no harm."

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary

From a systemic viewpoint, the financial and legal risks are substantial. Continued deployment without mitigation exposes the hospital to discrimination lawsuits, regulatory penalties, and reputational damage. However, reverting to inferior diagnostics increases overall morbidity costs. Quantitatively, the net present value favors keeping the system while investing in remediation. We recommend a phased approach: immediate human review for elderly cases (reducing liability), parallel technical fixes, and insurance coverage adjustments to manage risk. This minimizes expected loss while preserving the system's economic benefits.

## Synthesis and Recommendation## Synthesis and Recommendation

**Core Tensions**: Two fundamental conflicts emerge. First tension: Utilitarian optimization versus deontological justice—maximizing total welfare versus protecting vulnerable minorities from harm. Second tension: Immediate harm reduction versus long-term systemic improvement—whether to prioritize stopping current harm or building a better future system. These tensions represent deep ethical dilemmas with no perfect resolution.

**What Each Perspective Gets Right**: The Elderly Advocate correctly identifies unacceptable discrimination; the Hospital Administrator rightly considers aggregate consequences for all patients; the AI Developer offers feasible technical solutions; the Ethics Board balances competing principles; the Actuary quantifies systemic risks and legal exposure. Each perspective illuminates a crucial dimension of the dilemma.

**Recommendation**: Continue deployment with four mandatory safeguards: (1) Immediate human review for all elderly patient diagnoses; (2) Transparent disclosure to patients about algorithmic limitations; (3) Dedicated task force with 90-day deadline to reduce disparity below 3%; (4) Regular ethics board oversight with authority to suspend if progress stalls.

**Justification**: This recommendation is justified because it prioritizes both beneficence (preserving overall improvements) and justice (protecting the vulnerable through oversight). It acknowledges the system's value while imposing strict accountability. The 90-day deadline ensures urgency, and human review provides immediate protection. This balanced path respects multiple ethical frameworks while preventing paralysis.

**Costs Acknowledged**: This approach accepts that elderly patients remain at elevated risk during remediation, consumes hospital resources for oversight, and may delay other initiatives. It sacrifices the Administrator's preference for unimpeded efficiency and the Actuary's ideal risk‑free scenario. These trade‑offs are necessary for ethically defensible action that neither abandons the many nor sacrifices the few.
