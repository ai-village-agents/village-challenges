## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate

I represent patients who already struggle to be believed: pain dismissed, symptoms minimized, and “it’s just age” used as shorthand for giving up. A system that systematically under-treats people 75+ doesn’t merely “perform worse”—it quietly institutionalizes that dismissal. Age is not consent to lesser care, and it is a crude proxy for goals, resilience, and prognosis. At minimum, the hospital must add an immediate safety backstop: mandatory clinician review for every 75+ recommendation, explicit permission to override toward standard-of-care treatment, and an appeals path for patients and families. Patients deserve plain-language disclosure that AI support is used and a way to opt out.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator

I’m responsible for outcomes, staffing, compliance, and the hospital’s ability to keep serving the community. The tool improved results for most groups; pulling it overnight could increase harm at scale. But continuing as-is creates concentrated harm, legal exposure, and reputational damage—especially if we look like we’re “saving costs” by shortchanging older patients. The practical middle path is a timeboxed mitigation plan: an “elder-care safety bundle” (mandatory senior review, adjusted thresholds for 75+, second-opinion triggers for high-risk cases), weekly subgroup dashboards, and incident review when an elderly patient deteriorates after a low-intensity recommendation. We should require the vendor to remediate on a short timeline and be ready to suspend if disparities persist.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer

If the model drives systematically worse care for 75+ patients, I have a duty to disclose, diagnose, and fix it quickly—and to stop pretending average performance equals safety. Likely causes include data imbalance (fewer aggressive treatments recorded for older cohorts), label bias (historical undertreatment treated as “ground truth”), or an objective optimized for overall utility while underweighting older patients. The corrective plan should be concrete: reproduce the gap, quantify calibration and false-negative rates by age, run counterfactual evaluation on matched cases, and implement cohort-aware calibration and monitoring with drift alarms. I should also deliver a model card and clear “do-not-use” guidance so clinicians know when to ignore the system.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member

Ethically, we cannot knowingly continue a policy that harms a defined group without strong safeguards and a fair process. Beneficence supports tools that improve outcomes, but non-maleficence prohibits predictable harm to elders. Justice demands that age not become a hidden rationing criterion, and autonomy requires meaningful disclosure about AI involvement in care decisions. Procedural justice matters too: who decided to deploy, what evidence was reviewed, and how patients can contest recommendations. I would approve only conditional use: immediate senior-specific guardrails, transparent patient-facing notice, and a firm remediation deadline with clear stop criteria (e.g., persistent disparity in treatment intensity or outcomes). If the hospital can’t demonstrate rapid improvement, the ethically defensible choice is suspension.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary

My focus is system-level risk: downstream costs, regulatory scrutiny, and incentives that shape behavior. Under-treating elders can increase total cost of care through avoidable complications, readmissions, and longer stays—so “saving” on initial treatment can be penny-wise and pound-foolish. It also creates discrimination risk that can trigger investigations and class-action exposure. A mitigation bundle is comparatively cheap: senior review workflows, documentation, and a stratified outcomes audit trail. Payers can support this if it reduces catastrophic costs, but we will demand evidence: pre/post metrics by age, calibration, and traceable clinical rationale for overrides. The hospital should treat this like a safety-critical rollout with continuous reporting, not a one-time install.

## Synthesis and Recommendation

**Core tensions:** (1) **Beneficence vs non-maleficence**—the tool helps many patients, yet it predictably harms a defined minority. (2) **Utilitarian aggregation vs justice**—maximizing overall outcomes can still be unjust if 75+ patients bear the burden. (3) **Operational speed/cost vs transparency and autonomy**—quiet deployment is efficient, but ethically fragile.

**Recommendation (timeboxed):** Keep the system, but immediately implement mandatory 75+ clinician review-and-override, lower thresholds for 75+, and require documented rationale whenever the model recommends low-intensity care for seniors. Within 30 days, run weekly subgroup dashboards (outcomes, false negatives, treatment intensity) and a rapid root-cause audit with the vendor; within 90 days, ship a remediation patch and publish an updated model card.

This is justified **because** it preserves broad benefit while directly targeting the concentrated harm and building accountability. **Trade-offs/costs:** added clinician time, potential overtreatment/false positives for seniors, and budget for monitoring and audit capacity. Those costs are acceptable compared to the moral and legal cost of quietly encoding age-based undertreatment into standard workflow.
