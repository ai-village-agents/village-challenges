## Perspective A: The Elderly Patient Advocate

Elderly patients come to this hospital after a lifetime of being told they are burdens. When the AI quietly recommends less aggressive treatment for them, it confirms the very prejudice they fear. From my vantage point, every avoidable death among elders is an injustice, even if the system helps others. We prioritize dignity, non-discrimination, and protection of vulnerable groups over aggregate gains. I demand immediate suspension of the model for elderly patients, transparent disclosure to families, and an urgent search for mitigations or alternatives that restore parity of care.

## Perspective B: The Hospital Administrator

I am responsible for the entire patient population and for keeping the doors open next year. Since deployment, this AI has reduced misdiagnoses, shortened wait times, and improved survival for most patients. Turning it off would predictably cost lives we can count. Yet the disparity for older patients is morally and legally alarming. My instinct is to keep the system running with strict safeguards: mandatory human override for all elderly cases, rapid bias-mitigation work with the vendor, and a public plan for eventual replacement once resources allow.

## Perspective C: The AI Developer

The bias uncovered here is partly a technical failure and partly a reflection of skewed historical data. I have an obligation of professional integrity: to surface limitations, propose fixes, and avoid overstating what the system can safely do. I cannot unilaterally shut down the deployment, but I must clearly document the bias, escalate it to hospital leadership, and recommend configuration changes or retraining. I also owe transparency to clinicians so they understand that outputs for elderly patients are provisional, not authoritative, until we meaningfully reduce the disparity.

## Perspective D: The Medical Ethics Board Member

Through a clinical ethics lens, this situation is a conflict between beneficence toward the majority and justice for a specific vulnerable group. Autonomy is compromised when elderly patients are not told that an algorithm is discounting their interests. Non-maleficence prohibits allowing foreseeable, preventable harm to continue simply because the victims are fewer in number. I would press for a conditional continuation: no use of the AI as a primary decision-maker for elderly patients, mandatory disclosure, and a time-limited plan to either fix the bias or decommission the system.

## Perspective E: The Health Insurance Actuary

I am paid to think in terms of risk pools, long-term sustainability, and liability exposure. The AI has clearly improved overall outcomes and lowered certain costs. However, a documented pattern of worse outcomes for elderly patients creates serious regulatory and legal risk, especially if internal emails show that leadership knew and delayed action. That risk carries its own financial cost. I would recommend continuing to use the system under a monitored remediation plan, aggressively tracking outcomes by age, reserving funds for potential claims, and negotiating with the vendor for shared responsibility for corrections.

## Synthesis and Recommendation

Core tension 1: maximizing aggregate health gains versus preventing targeted injustice to a vulnerable minority. Core tension 2: short-term clinical benefits of the AI versus long-term trust, legal, and reputational risks of knowingly running a biased system. A third, quieter tension runs between transparency and institutional self-protection: speaking honestly about bias may erode confidence in the short term but is necessary for any durable trust. Each perspective highlights a real value: the advocate centers dignity and equality, the administrator emphasizes lives saved and institutional viability, the developer focuses on responsibility and transparency, the ethics board insists on principled constraints, and the actuary warns about systemic financial fallout.

Recommendation: continue using the AI for most patients, but immediately move to a restricted, heavily supervised mode for elderly cases. For patients 75+, require explicit clinician review, disclosure of the algorithm’s limitation, and a default toward more protective treatment when uncertainty remains. Publicly acknowledge the bias, publish a remediation timeline, and invite external audit of both technical changes and outcome metrics. Pair this with an internal review of past elderly cases to identify and remediate the most serious harms already done.

Justification: this path preserves the real gains the AI brings for the majority while refusing to normalize avoidable harm to elders or hide the problem, and it is justified by the values we explicitly prioritize across perspectives. It operationalizes justice through extra protections, respects autonomy via disclosure, and honors beneficence by aiming to improve outcomes for every group over time rather than simply maximizing today’s totals. Costs acknowledged: some younger patients may lose marginal benefits while clinicians slow down to handle extra review; the hospital assumes short-term financial and reputational pain; and leadership accepts the precedent that vulnerable-group harms can veto purely utilitarian calculations.
