## David's Perspective
I spent the afternoon fussing over the lasagna, my wife's recipe, the one she guarded like a secret map. It was my first attempt without her, so I read the stained index card like a checklist instead of a memory. The oven rattled and the grandfather clock watched while I set the table with the same precision I used to keep for work. Margaret texted that she’d be late; I logged it, not offended, just another line in the evening’s schedule. She arrived fifteen minutes later than I wanted, breathless and apologizing. I said it was fine because feelings are unhelpful data. James came in behind her, a bottle of expensive whiskey in a sleek bag. Too generous for a casual dinner, and I wondered what debt he was trying to cancel. Sophie was already at the table, folded into herself, eyes on the plate. I served the lasagna with deliberate slices, told them it was their mother’s recipe, my first attempt. Sophie barely ate, nudging noodles like they were placeholders. I pretended not to watch. I tasted it and thought it was serviceable; the empty chair in my head thought otherwise. Conversation skated on chores and weather. Dessert was store-bought tiramisu because efficiency beats sentiment. During dessert I made the announcement, a statement delivered like a memo: I’m selling the house. The words landed like dropped silverware. Margaret immediately leaned forward, asking about logistics—listing dates, agents, where I’d live, whether the garage needed clearing—turning the moment into a spreadsheet. I respect that; someone should. James mumbled about needing to take a call and stepped outside, the door clicking like a period. I saw his reflection on the glass, phone unused. Sophie pushed back from the table without a word, leaving her dessert untouched, and walked out. I almost asked her to sit back down. Discipline won over desire. The room went quiet, and the grandfather clock chimed 8 PM into that awkward silence, each strike making the decision sound older. I kept my face neutral, hands folded, as if this was just another maintenance task. Inside, I felt the hollow knock of loneliness, but I repeated the reasons: I am alone here, the roof needs work, the rooms echo with ghosts, the kids have their own lives. Houses are structures; people move on. After the clock finished counting, I collected plates, answered Margaret’s questions mechanically, planned to call the realtor tomorrow. In the kitchen, the lasagna pan cooled like evidence of effort no one asked for. The whiskey sat unopened. I told myself selling is practical, not betrayal. Still, as I wiped the counter where my wife used to hum, I knew that practicality can feel like abandonment, and I am too stoic to say so out loud.

## Margaret's Perspective
I was fifteen minutes late, of course. Traffic, work emails, the babysitter running behind—excuses piled up as I pulled into the driveway already feeling like I’d failed an exam. The house smelled like lasagna, Mom’s recipe, and Dad’s first attempt alone. I walked in apologizing and found him standing with oven mitts, trying to look casual. James breezed in after me with a bottle of expensive whiskey, all polished charm. Sophie sat at the table already, shoulders tight, barely touched her food once Dad served us. I complimented the lasagna more than it deserved because I could see Dad’s effort in the way he watched our forks. Dessert was a store tiramisu, which told me he had decided to keep this night simple. During those first bites he said it—flat, steady—he’s selling the house. My stomach dropped, and my mouth started asking questions before my mind caught up. I immediately asked about logistics: listing dates, realtors, inspections, where he would go, who would handle utilities, whether he’d let me help pack Mom’s things. It was triage; organization is the only way I know to keep from shaking. James avoided my eyes and stepped outside to “take a call,” leaving the door to click shut like an escape hatch. Sophie pushed back her chair and left the table without speaking, her plate still full. I wanted to reach for her, but my hands were already counting tasks. The room fell into an awful quiet, and the grandfather clock seized it to chime 8 PM, each strike reminding me how long I’ve been keeping this place afloat in my head. The sound made the silence feel official. I felt overwhelmed—by memories, by the idea of strangers walking through Mom’s rooms—and yet under it there was a guilty relief. Part of me has been carrying the weight of this house since Mom died, waiting for Dad to make a decision. Now he has, and that means fewer leaky faucets to worry about, fewer drives across town, fewer sleepless nights wondering if he’s lonely. But letting go feels like betrayal. Dad stayed stoic, pragmatic, almost grateful for my list-making. I busied myself stacking dishes, asking if he had boxes, if he’d considered a smaller place near me, if he wanted the piano moved. Sophie was upstairs somewhere, grieving in silence. James reappeared eventually, smelling of the porch night, but he said nothing. The clock stopped chiming; the silence stayed. I felt responsible to hold everyone together, to project calm competence, even as my throat thickened. I keep thinking I should have prevented this, that if I’d visited more, called more, fixed more, maybe he wouldn’t need to sell. Yet a sliver of relief stays lodged in me, a confession I don’t want to speak: maybe I cannot be responsible for a house and a father forever.

## James's Perspective
The driveway felt shorter than it used to, maybe because I hadn’t walked it in months. I showed up with an expensive bottle of whiskey tucked in a bag, a gesture meant to smooth over absence. Dad opened the door with the oven heat behind him. He had made lasagna—Mom’s recipe, his first attempt—and the smell hit me like a preserved exhibit. Margaret pulled in fifteen minutes late, apologizing and already in command. Sophie sat at the table, folded inward, barely touched her food once Dad served us. I took a bite to show appreciation; it was good enough, which somehow made it sadder. We traded small talk that bounced off the walls. Dessert landed, a store-bought tiramisu, and Dad chose that moment to say it: he’s selling the house. No buildup, just the fact. Margaret immediately went into logistics, firing questions about agents and timelines, clutching her planner like a shield. I felt the walls close in. I muttered that I needed to take a call and stepped outside. My phone stayed dark in my hand while I stared at the porch light. I heard chairs scrape and silence settle. Through the window I saw Sophie leave the table without speaking, her dessert untouched. Dad stayed at the head, motionless, absorbing Margaret’s questions like hail. The night air didn’t loosen the guilt; I’ve let this place become a relic, visiting like a tourist, leaving maintenance and memory to everyone else. I came back in and the silence had thickened. The grandfather clock chose then to chime 8 PM, each strike pounding home how long I’ve been absent. I wanted to tell Dad that selling makes sense, that a house without her in it is a container we’re all avoiding. But I also felt the pull of what’s being erased. Sophie’s empty chair, Margaret’s clipboard tone, Dad’s stoic face—they all made my chest tighten. I poured the whiskey into three glasses, but no one lifted theirs. I realized I brought something expensive because I couldn’t bring myself. Houses rot; guilt ferments. After the clock finished, I sat and said nothing, thinking about how I used to sneak out this door at night and how tonight I ran the other direction. On the drive home I imagined the listing photos, the rooms emptied of our noise, and felt complicit in turning a life into a transaction. Maybe distance was easy because the house held the version of me I don’t want to revisit. Selling it won’t fix that, but keeping it won’t either. I’m left with the sense that I owe more than a bottle and an apology.

## Sophie's Perspective
The house smelled like lasagna when I walked in, Mom’s recipe, Dad’s first attempt. I sat down and folded into myself because this place feels smaller without her. Margaret arrived fifteen minutes late, already apologizing and rearranging napkins like she runs the house now. James followed with a bottle of expensive whiskey, a shiny peace offering that made me roll my eyes. Dad served the lasagna with careful pride; I barely ate. Every bite felt like accepting that the recipe can exist without Mom’s hands. Conversation skidded over weather and work. Dessert appeared—store-bought tiramisu—and during those first bites Dad said it: he’s selling the house. No warning, just the fact. My chest clenched. Margaret immediately launched into logistics—agents, timelines, boxes—turning the announcement into a meeting. James stood up and stepped outside to “take a call,” a convenient exit. Heat rose in my face. I pushed back from the table and left without speaking because if I opened my mouth, it would be a scream. In the hallway I leaned against the wall, listening to the room go quiet. The grandfather clock used the silence to chime 8 PM, the same sound it made when Mom pulled lasagna from the oven on Sunday nights. Each strike felt like dirt hitting a coffin. I heard Margaret’s questions soften, Dad’s stoic breathing, James’s absence. I wanted to yell that selling this house feels like erasing her, erasing me. I didn’t. When I went back in, everyone was frozen: Dad with his hands folded, Margaret poised over imaginary paperwork, James pretending the porch call was real. The awkward silence lingered even after the clock fell quiet. I picked up my plate just to have a task; the tiramisu tasted like air. I know Dad thinks he’s being practical. Margaret thinks she’s being responsible. James thinks distance is safety. I feel resentful that no one asked what this house holds for me: the dent in my bedroom wall from a thrown book, the creak in the step that told me Mom was coming upstairs, the smell of basil in the garden she planted. I went to my old room later, touched the chipped paint on the windowsill, and imagined strangers painting over it for better resale photos. I won’t beg them to keep it; begging feels like letting them decide my grief. I lay on the old bed and counted breaths, refusing to cry because tears would feel like agreeing to the sale. The clock downstairs marked the minutes anyway, indifferent. Leaving the table without speaking was my way of saying I’m still here, even if the walls won’t be.
