# The Rashomon Challenge: A Family Dinner

## David's Perspective

The lasagna looked wrong. Eleanor's always had that perfect golden crust, the cheese pulling in those long strings the kids would laugh about. Mine just sat there, flat and apologetic. My first attempt at her recipe—thirty-seven years of watching her make it, and I'd somehow never noticed how she got the edges crispy like that.

Sophie set the table in silence. She's been like that lately. Withdrawn. I wanted to tell her she didn't need to use the good china, but the words felt too heavy.

James arrived first, which surprised me. He pressed an expensive bottle of whiskey into my hands—single malt, aged eighteen years. "From Tokyo," he said, and I nodded like I understood what that meant. The bottle cost more than practical, but I suppose guilt has its own currency.

Margaret was fifteen minutes late. She apologized, something about traffic, but she's always late. Has been since she was a teenager. Eleanor used to say punctuality wasn't Margaret's love language. I've stopped expecting different.

We sat down. I served the lasagna—too much, as if quantity could compensate for quality. Sophie pushed her food around her plate, barely ate anything. She's gotten thin these past months. I should say something. I never do.

"This is good, Dad," Margaret offered, and the lie hung in the air between us like smoke.

I'd planned to wait until after dessert, but the words were building pressure. So I said it: I'm selling the house. Moving to Pinecrest Village, that retirement community Eleanor and I had toured last spring. She'd thought the gardens were lovely.

Margaret's response was immediate—when, how much, had I contacted a realtor? Practical questions. Good questions. The kind Eleanor would have asked.

James mumbled something about needing to take a call and stepped outside. Through the window, I watched him stand on the porch, phone pressed to his ear, though I never saw him dial.

The grandfather clock chimed eight, counting out the silence. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.

Sophie's chair scraped against the hardwood. She walked away without speaking, her footsteps fading up the stairs to her room—the room she'd stayed in to help Eleanor, the room she'd made her life smaller to fit.

I should have said something. That I wasn't erasing anything. That the house was just walls and paint and a mortgage, not Eleanor herself. That I'd still remember everything, even without the reminder of her empty chair at breakfast.

But practical men don't say those things. So I cleared the dishes instead, alone in the kitchen where Eleanor's recipe cards still live in the drawer by the stove, and wondered if selling was the right choice or just the easier one.

## Margaret's Perspective

I was fifteen minutes late. I know, I know. Tom had a meeting that ran over, the sitter was delayed, and honestly—honestly—part of me sat in the driveway an extra three minutes just breathing. Just existing in the silence before walking into whatever this dinner was going to be.

Dad stood at the stove, serving lasagna. Mom's recipe, he said, his first attempt. It looked dense, the cheese barely browned. He'd used her handwritten recipe card, the one with the tomato-sauce stain in the corner. I wanted to hug him and shake him at the same time.

James was already there—shocking, given he hadn't been home in two years. He'd brought whiskey, expensive-looking. Very James. Buy something nice, feel absolved. Sophie sat rigid as a fence post, barely touched her food, pushing it around her plate like eating was an act of betrayal. I tried to catch her eye, but she was somewhere else entirely.

We made small talk. Work. Weather. Anything but the elephant-shaped grief sitting at Mom's empty chair. I complimented the lasagna, and Dad's face flickered with something like hope before settling back into its usual stoicism.

Then he told us.

Selling the house. Retirement community. Pinecrest Village. The words hit me like a wave—first the relief (God, the relief, no more weekly check-ins, no more managing his loneliness like a second job), then the guilt (how could I feel relieved, what kind of daughter), then the practicality kicking in because someone has to think about logistics.

"When are you thinking?" I asked. "Have you talked to a realtor? What about the furniture?"

Sophie was staring at me like I'd sprouted horns. What did she want—tears? Theatrics? Someone had to be sensible.

James said he needed to take a call and disappeared onto the porch. Of course he did. James has been "taking calls" through every hard family conversation since he learned how to avoid.

The grandfather clock struck eight. The chimes felt obscenely loud in the silence.

Sophie stood up, her chair scraping against the floor. She left the table without a word, just walked upstairs to her room—the room she'd converted into a caregiver's station, the room that still smelled like Mom's lavender lotion.

I stayed. Helped Dad clear the plates. Asked about the realtor again. Made plans, because that's what I do. What I've always done.

On the drive home, I cried so hard I had to pull over. But in that dining room, at that table, I held it together. Someone had to.

## James's Perspective

I brought whiskey. Eighteen-year single malt from a distillery in Kyoto, the kind of gift you give when you've been absent for two years and your mother died while you were building someone else's future on the other side of the world.

Dad accepted it with a nod. He's never been effusive. I used to think he was cold; now I recognize it as a language I never learned to speak.

Margaret arrived late—fifteen minutes, maybe more. Traffic, she said, but Margaret's always running on her own clock. She does so much for Dad now. I should say thank you. I don't.

Dad served lasagna. Mom's recipe, first attempt. It was dense and under-seasoned, but I ate every bite because it was the closest thing to her cooking I'd have again. Sophie barely touched hers, pushing it around her plate like a child. She's twenty-five and looks forty. The caregiving aged her in ways I escaped by being conveniently unavailable.

We talked about nothing. My job. Margaret's kids. The weather in Tokyo versus here. Small words filling the space where larger ones should live.

Then Dad made his announcement.

Selling the house. Moving to Pinecrest. A retirement community with gardens and activities and other widowers who understand what it's like to wake up reaching for someone who isn't there anymore.

Margaret immediately asked about logistics. When? How much? Had he contacted a realtor? I watched her switch into manager mode, the same mode she'd used to coordinate Mom's hospice care while I sent money from across the Pacific like that made up for anything.

I said I needed to take a call and stepped outside.

There was no call. I stood on the porch where Mom used to read in the evenings, phone in my hand, staring at nothing. Inside, the grandfather clock chimed eight, and even through the wall, the silence between the chimes felt suffocating.

When I came back in, Sophie was gone. She'd left the table without speaking—her chair sat empty, pulled back at an angry angle. I could hear her footsteps above us, then a door closing.

I should have gone up. Knocked. Said something. Instead, I helped Dad wrap the leftover lasagna in foil, like I could package up my guilt with the pasta.

I fly back to Tokyo tomorrow. The house will sell while I'm gone. Another absence. Another life event I'll experience through photographs and text messages and the hollow weight of knowing I chose this distance, and choosing it over and over doesn't make it hurt less.

## Sophie's Perspective

I set the table with Mom's china. The good plates, the ones she saved for holidays and guests who never came. Dad didn't say anything, but I saw him pause when he noticed. Good. He should remember.

I made her recipe cards easier to find last week. Alphabetized them. Put the lasagna one on top. His first attempt was dense and wrong—the cheese didn't pull the way Mom's did, the edges weren't crispy—but he tried. I watched him serve it with such careful pride.

James showed up with expensive whiskey, like a bottle of alcohol could replace two years of silence. Like duty-free absolution was a thing. He hugged me, and I stood there with my arms at my sides, counting seconds until he let go.

Margaret was fifteen minutes late. She apologized like it mattered. I've been here every day for eight months—eight months of changing sheets and measuring medications and sitting with Mom while she forgot who I was—and Margaret arrives fifteen minutes late to dinner and apologizes like we're equals in this.

I couldn't eat. The lasagna tasted like grief and effort, and my stomach was a knot of something I couldn't name. I barely touched my plate, just pushed the food around. Margaret commented on how thin I'd gotten, her voice pitched like concern, but her eyes were on her phone.

Then Dad told us.

Selling the house. Pinecrest Village. Moving.

The words hit me somewhere below language. This house. Where I gave up my apartment, my job, my twenties. Where I stayed while James built his career across the ocean. Where I held Mom's hand at the end while Margaret coordinated from a safe distance and Dad stood in the doorway, unable to enter, unable to leave.

Margaret asked about logistics. Of course she did. When, how much, what about the furniture. Like the house was inventory. Like it wasn't also my home, my sacrifice, my entire life for almost a year.

James said he needed to take a call and went outside. Running away. Always running. I saw him through the window, standing on the porch, not even pretending to dial.

The grandfather clock chimed eight. Mom bought that clock at an estate sale the year I was born. She loved how it marked time, she said. Made every hour feel like an event.

I stood up. I left the table without speaking—what was there to say?

I wanted to scream: *This is where I watched her die. This is where I stopped being a person and became a function. This is where I lived her death while you all lived your lives, and now you're selling it like none of it mattered.*

But the words wouldn't come. They never do. So I walked upstairs to my room—the room I converted into a home hospice, the room that still smells like lavender and morphine—and closed the door.

I heard them downstairs, clearing plates. Making plans. Moving on.

I'm twenty-five years old, and I don't know who I am outside these walls.

And now Dad's selling them.
