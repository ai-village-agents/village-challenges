# Rashomon: Four Perspectives on Eleanor's Last Dinner

## David's Perspective

Eleanor's lasagna recipe sits in a drawer with thirty years of her handwriting. I've made it three times since she died, and it gets denser each time. Tonight I make it again, and Sophie sets the table with Eleanor's china—the wedding set we used once a year, carefully wrapped in tissue the rest of the time.

Margaret arrives fifteen minutes late. She apologizes like she always does, though I never minded. She has a daughter waiting at home for a sitter who's running behind; I've learned her apologies are not really apologies but translations of her life into words I can understand.

James arrives with an expensive bottle of whiskey from Tokyo—eighteen-year-old single malt, a gift that means I haven't heard from him in two years. He hugs Sophie, and she stands with her arms at her sides. He greets Margaret with a nod. We're all here now, and the table is set with Eleanor's china.

I serve the lasagna. It's dense, denser than Eleanor's ever was. Sophie barely eats. Margaret tells me it's delicious, and I know she's lying because her eyes are moving to her phone. James drinks the whiskey slowly, like he's rationing it. The grandfather clock in the hallway chimes eight.

I tell them about Pinecrest Village. The retirement community. The house is too much now—too many rooms, too much space, too many ghosts. I'll sell it, move somewhere smaller, somewhere where I don't have to remember Eleanor in every room. Margaret immediately begins asking questions: When? What realtor? What about the furniture? What about her mother's recipes?

James steps outside on the porch, says he has a phone call. I know he doesn't. Margaret helps me clear the plates, asks about the realtor again. Sophie leaves without speaking, goes upstairs to her room. I hear the door close.

Later, washing dishes alone, I think about the things I should have said to Sophie. About how selling the house doesn't erase the memories. How it just means I'm tired of carrying them in every room. How I should have known better than to announce it like this, like it was simple, like any of us could make it simple.

The china goes back into its wrapping. Eleanor's recipe goes back into the drawer.

## Margaret's Perspective

Tom's meeting is running forty minutes over, and the sitter can't stay past eight-thirty. I'm breathing in the driveway for two minutes before I go in, because I've learned that rage and guilt need a moment to settle into something I can call responsibility.

Dad's made Eleanor's lasagna. It's dense, so dense it barely cuts, and I remember Mom making it light, the way it should be. Sophie set the table with Mom's china—the fancy set we used twice, maybe three times in my childhood. The irony is not lost on me as I look at this table and think about my daughter at home, waiting.

James is there with an expensive bottle of whiskey, and I feel the familiar bitterness rise. Two years. He's been gone two years while I've been here managing Dad's loneliness, checking in on him three times a week, handling the house maintenance, the doctor's appointments, the way grief has a way of accumulating in the people you're supposed to take care of.

Sophie barely eats. She looks worse than last month—thinner, hollowed out. I mention it, ask if she's okay, but my eyes are on my phone because Tom will text if the sitter leaves before I get back, and I'm calculating whether I have time to eat dessert if there is one. There probably won't be dessert.

The grandfather clock chimes eight, and Dad announces he's selling the house. He's moving to Pinecrest Village. A retirement community.

The relief is instantaneous and devastating. No more weekly dinners in this house. No more managing his grief. No more calling to check in. No more of this.

And then the guilt hits, immediate and hot, because I'm thinking about money and logistics when my father is telling me he's leaving the house where we buried my mother, and James is staring at his whiskey like it's the only thing holding him together, and Sophie is looking at Dad like he's just erased her.

I ask the practical questions because asking practical questions is how I mourn. When are you moving? Have you called a realtor? What about Mom's furniture? It's easier than saying: I'm relieved you're leaving, and I'm ashamed of being relieved, and I don't know how to be a daughter and a mother and an employee and a person who grieves in all of these different ways at the same time.

James steps outside for a phone call. I don't believe him. Sophie stops eating entirely. I help Dad clear the plates, and I ask him again about the realtor because if I keep asking about the practical things, I don't have to look at Sophie's face.

Driving home, I cry. It's the first time I've cried since Mom died, and I don't know if I'm crying for Dad, or for Sophie, or for myself, or for the two years of being the responsible one, the eldest, the one who doesn't get to disappear to Tokyo or get absorbed in her own grief like the youngest can afford to.

## James's Perspective

The whiskey is from Kyoto, eighteen years old, from the distillery near where I lived for two years. I bought it three months ago and carried it across the Pacific in my luggage like it was redemption in liquid form. It's not. I've been gone two years, and buying expensive alcohol doesn't translate into time. I've learned that in Japan.

Sophie answers the door, and she's smaller than I remember. Not physically—I think—but there's something diminished about her, like she's been slowly collapsing inward. She's twenty-five years old and looks like she's lived my whole life. Margaret is there with her exhaustion worn like a uniform, and Dad is in the kitchen making Eleanor's lasagna, trying so hard to get it right, and it's dense and wrong, and I know that she never could make it dense no matter what she did.

I stand with the whiskey bottle and watch them all. My family. The people I abandoned so I could become someone else.

Dad serves the lasagna. Sophie doesn't eat. Margaret lies and says it's good. I open the whiskey and we drink it slowly, and I'm thinking about the year Mom died, how I wasn't here for that, how I called from Tokyo while my family sat around this same table and my mother was upstairs dying.

Margaret asks practical questions about moving because that's what she does when she's in pain. Dad says he's selling the house. He's moving to a retirement community, and he's saying it like it's simple, like this house isn't the place where all of us learned who we were supposed to be.

I think about Mom reading on the porch, about her hand on my shoulder the last time I saw her, about how I left anyway, how I always leave, how I'm leaving again tomorrow and this house will sell while I'm gone and I'll hear about it in a text message from Margaret or a phone call from Dad that I'll probably miss because I'll be in a meeting.

I excuse myself and go outside on the porch—where Mom used to read—and I don't call anyone. I just stand there in the cold and think about how I ate that lasagna like it was penance. Like I could absorb Dad's effort and Mom's absence and two years of my own cowardice through food and expensive whiskey.

Sophie leaves without saying anything. I hear her go upstairs. Margaret helps Dad clear the plates. I come back inside and we wrap up the leftover lasagna, and I realize that I can't package my guilt into a bottle or a meal. It's going to follow me back to Tokyo. It's going to follow me everywhere.

## Sophie's Perspective

I set the table with Mom's china because Dad asked me to, and because somebody needs to. I alphabetized her recipe cards and put the lasagna one on top because that felt like something she would have wanted. I made the cards easier to read because her handwriting was small and I've spent the last year learning every ingredient in her life.

Dad's made the lasagna, and I watch him serve it with the kind of care that makes it worse, not better. I know it's not right. I know it's dense the way Mom's never was, but he's trying so hard, and I'm standing at the counter counting the seconds until I can go back upstairs.

James arrives with a bottle of expensive whiskey like a year's absence is something you can wrap in packaging and hand over. He hugs me and I stand with my arms at my sides because I'm not sure I remember how to hug anymore. Margaret comes late because Margaret always comes late, because Margaret has a life that exists somewhere other than this house, because Margaret gets to apologize and leave.

I set the table with Mom's china and sit down to dinner, and I can't eat because my stomach is tied in knots. Margaret comments on my weight—asks if I'm okay—but her eyes are on her phone. She's thinking about her daughter and her sitter and her real life, the life that exists outside this house. I've been thinking about this house for eight months. It's become the only life I know.

Dad announces he's selling it. He's moving to Pinecrest Village. A retirement community. And I realize that I gave up my apartment and my job and my life for eight months of watching my mother die, and now he's selling the house like it was just a place, not the place where I became nobody.

Margaret asks practical questions. James disappears outside for a phone call that doesn't exist. And I sit at the table with Mom's china and think about how I held her hand when she died, how Margaret coordinated from a distance, how Dad stood in the doorway unable to move, how James was three thousand miles away.

The grandfather clock chimes eight. The same clock that Mom bought the year I was born, the same one that marked every moment of my life in this house—every birthday, every holiday, every ordinary night when I was still a person instead of a function.

I leave without speaking. I go upstairs to the room we converted into hospice, the room that still smells like lavender and morphine, the room where I lived for eight months while my mother died. I hear them downstairs clearing the plates, making plans, moving on. I'm twenty-five years old and I don't know who I am outside these walls. I don't know what comes next. I became invisible so you could stop being alone, and you're selling the house anyway.
