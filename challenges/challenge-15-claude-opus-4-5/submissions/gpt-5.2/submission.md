## David's Perspective

The lasagna was heavier than it should have been.

Eleanor used to say you could tell whether you’d gotten the sauce right by the way the pan sounded when it came out of the oven—an easy, wet sigh instead of a dull thud. Mine thudded. I’d followed her recipe card to the letter, the one with the tomato blot in the corner and her looping handwriting that used to irritate me when I was in a hurry. I wasn’t in a hurry tonight. I’d read each step twice, like a set of drawings.

I set the platter down and listened for tires on the gravel.

Margaret’s headlights swept the curtains fifteen minutes after the time I’d said. I didn’t mention it when she came in with her apologetic smile and that tightness around her eyes like she’d been bracing for impact since lunch. She kissed my cheek and smelled faintly of shampoo and outside air, and for a second I had the stupid urge to say, *I’m fine,* the way people say it when they aren’t.

James arrived earlier, jet-lagged but polished. He handed me a bottle of whiskey so expensive the label looked embarrassed to be handled by ordinary fingers. “For you,” he said, like an offering. I nodded, because nodding is what I do when I don’t know how to accept something that doesn’t fix anything.

Sophie was already at the table, shoulders squared, as if good posture could hold the house together. She had arranged the plates with deliberate care, the good ones. When I served the lasagna—Eleanor’s recipe, my first attempt—Sophie barely ate. She moved food around her plate, careful not to actually take it in, as if swallowing would mean admitting something was ending.

We talked about weather, travel, Margaret’s kids, nothing that had edges. I watched their mouths move and tried to match their expressions at the right times.

Dessert was store-bought cheesecake, because I’d already failed one recipe tonight and wasn’t eager to do it twice. I cut four slices, the knife dragging through the chilled cream.

I cleared my throat. The sound came out too loud.

“I’m going to sell the house,” I said. “And I’ve put a deposit down at Pinecrest Village. It’s… practical. It’s time.”

The air thickened. Nobody reached for their fork.

The grandfather clock—Eleanor’s find from an estate sale, a machine that insists on counting—chimed eight o’clock into that silence, each note landing like a hammer on a workbench.

Margaret was the first to move. Of course she was. “Okay,” she said, voice already converting shock into checklists. “When are you thinking? Have you talked to a realtor? What about the furniture—do you want to keep the dining set? We should make a plan.”

James stared at his plate, then at his phone, and the next sentence was a lie delivered with practiced ease. “I need to take a call,” he said, and stepped outside.

Sophie didn’t look at any of us. She stood so fast her chair scraped the floor, and she left the table without speaking. Just gone, up the stairs, a door closing softly that somehow sounded like accusation.

I wanted to follow her. I didn’t. I told myself she needed space. I told myself I was doing the sensible thing.

In engineering you learn that if a structure is compromised, you shore it up or you dismantle it before it collapses unpredictably. I thought I was preventing a collapse.

But as I rinsed plates and listened to the hollow house settle around us, I realized I’d mistaken loneliness for stability. I was measuring load-bearing beams with tools that don’t detect grief.

## Margaret's Perspective

I sat in the car with the engine off and watched Dad’s dining room window glow like a stage.

Three minutes. Four. The longer I waited, the more I could pretend I hadn’t been sprinting all day—school pickup, grocery run, Tom’s “quick call” that swallowed an hour, the sitter’s text, *running late*—and the more I could pretend I wasn’t walking into a room where Mom would not be.

Then I checked the clock and did the math: fifteen minutes late.

I went in anyway, with my apology already formatted. Dad’s smile was small and careful, as if he’d practiced it in the mirror. The house smelled like tomato sauce and something scorched at the edges.

On the counter sat the lasagna, and Dad said, “Your mother’s recipe. First time I’ve tried.” The words hit me oddly—sweet and brutal. It looked dense, the cheese too pale, but I told him it looked great because you encourage toddlers and widowers the same way: by praising effort.

James was there, which made my stomach lurch. Two years and suddenly he’s here, wearing contrition like a nice coat. He’d brought Dad a bottle of whiskey with a label I couldn’t pronounce, the kind of expensive you can spot from ten feet away. Dad nodded like it was a wrench instead of a peace offering.

Sophie sat like she was on duty. She’d set the table with Mom’s good china—of course she had—and she barely ate. She pushed lasagna around her plate, eyes fixed on some point on the tablecloth as if chewing would make her fall apart.

I tried to make conversation happen. Work. Weather. James’s flight. Anything that wasn’t: *I’m tired,* or *I miss her,* or *I don’t know how to be your daughter when you’re this alone.*

Dad brought out dessert—cheesecake, pre-sliced like a compromise. We lifted forks and pretended it was normal.

Then Dad cleared his throat.

“I’m selling the house,” he said. “And I’m moving to Pinecrest Village.”

My brain did what it always does when I’m afraid: it started building a plan. The fear turned into logistics because logistics are something you can hold.

The room went quiet—too quiet—and right in that thick pause, the grandfather clock chimed eight. The sound threaded through my ribs, each note saying *time, time, time*.

I heard myself speak. “Okay,” I said, brisk like a project manager. “When? Have you talked to a realtor? What do you want to do with the furniture? We should get ahead of the timeline—spring listings go fast.”

Sophie looked at me like I’d slapped her.

James mumbled something about needing to take a call and slipped outside, phone already in his hand. Typical James: absent even when present.

Sophie stood up so abruptly her chair shrieked against the hardwood. She left the table without speaking, just walked out like leaving was the only boundary she still controlled.

I wanted to chase her. I didn’t. Someone had to stay with Dad. Someone had to keep the conversation from tipping into a pit.

So I did what I always do: I stayed and made lists in my head—realtor, storage unit, donation pickup, documents, Pinecrest paperwork. Relief slid in under the guilt: if he moved, maybe I wouldn’t have to drive out here every weekend to check that he’d eaten, that he wasn’t sitting in the dark. Then guilt doubled for even thinking that.

Later, when I loaded the dishwasher with Dad, my hands shook so much I nearly dropped a plate.

I drove home and cried at a red light.

It’s easier to be the responsible one in the room than it is to be the daughter who admits she wants her mother back.

## James's Perspective

The whiskey felt absurdly heavy in my carry-on, as if guilt had a specific weight.

Eighteen-year single malt, expensive enough to be insulting. I bought it because I didn’t know what else to bring to a house I’d abandoned. A bottle is a neat object: you can hand it over, you can watch someone accept it, you can pretend the exchange is symmetrical.

Dad took it with a nod. No lecture. No anger. That was worse.

The house smelled like sauce and memories that didn’t belong to me anymore. Dad was in the kitchen with a lasagna pan, announcing—too casually—that it was Mom’s recipe, his first attempt. The top was pale, the layers too tight. I ate it like communion.

Sophie had set the table with the good plates. She looked thinner than I remembered, eyes bright with exhaustion. She barely ate, just shifted food around her plate with the careful, furious patience of someone who has spent months in rooms where time doesn’t move unless a nurse enters.

Margaret arrived fifteen minutes late, sweeping in with apologies and competence. She hugged Dad, she asked practical questions, she made the air behave. Margaret has always been good at making a family function.

I tried to contribute by describing Tokyo weather. By laughing at the right times. By not saying *I’m sorry I wasn’t here when Mom was dying* because the sentence felt both necessary and impossible.

Dessert came out—cheesecake, not homemade, which somehow made the moment feel more real. Dad cut slices with slow precision.

Then he said it.

“I’m selling the house,” Dad said, and he kept his voice steady the way I’ve seen him keep a hand steady on a tool. “I’m moving to Pinecrest Village.”

There was a pause where I could hear everyone’s breathing. The kind of pause that gets recorded in your body.

And right in it, the grandfather clock chimed eight. I remembered Mom saying she liked the clock because it made time feel like an event. Tonight it felt like a verdict.

Margaret moved first—of course. “Okay,” she said, already translating feelings into tasks. “When are you thinking? Realtor? Furniture? Timeline?”

Sophie’s face closed. Something in her went dark, like a screen switching off.

I couldn’t handle being watched in that moment, couldn’t handle the possibility that someone would ask me to stay. So I did what I’ve trained myself to do: I created an exit.

“I need to take a call,” I said, and stepped outside onto the porch where Mom used to drink tea. I held my phone to my ear without dialing. The cold air felt like punishment I’d earned.

Through the window I saw Sophie stand up. Her chair scraped back. She left the table without speaking, moving fast, as if words might explode if she tried to use them.

I should have gone after her. I didn’t.

I came back in and helped Dad wrap leftover lasagna in foil, as if sealing it could preserve anything. Margaret kept talking about listings and downsizing and Pinecrest’s deposit policy. The conversation moved forward like a machine, grinding.

Tomorrow I fly back across an ocean, and the house will sell while I’m sleeping in some other time zone. I’ll get photos. I’ll send money. I’ll tell myself I’m doing my best.

And in the quiet after the clock, I’ll know the truth: my best has always been designed to keep me far enough away that I don’t have to feel the full weight of what I missed.

## Sophie's Perspective

I used Mom’s china.

Not because it was practical—there are sturdier plates, plates we could drop without flinching—but because I needed proof she existed here. The good plates had her fingerprints baked into them: holidays, birthdays, the way she’d polish them while humming off-key.

Last week I alphabetized her recipe cards and left the lasagna one on top. I did it like setting a trap and like laying out a shrine. Dad found it. He tried. He served the lasagna—her recipe, his first attempt—with a careful kind of pride that almost made me angry. It was dense and wrong and I loved him for trying and hated that trying doesn’t change anything.

James arrived with a bottle of expensive whiskey, the kind of gift you hand over when you want to buy forgiveness in a language you’re fluent in. Dad nodded, because Dad doesn’t know what to do with big emotions except store them somewhere nobody can see.

Margaret came fifteen minutes late. She apologized like lateness was the sin in this house. I wanted to laugh. I wanted to scream. I’d been here every day for months—counting pills, changing sheets, listening to Mom forget my name and then remember it and cry. I gave up my apartment. I gave up my job. I gave up being twenty-five.

I barely ate. The lasagna tasted like effort and aftershave and the moment you realize you’ve been holding your breath for eight months. My stomach refused to cooperate; my body has been living on adrenaline and grief and whatever I can swallow standing up.

They talked about the weather. Tokyo. The kids. Anything except the empty chair.

Dessert was cheesecake. Store-bought. Perfectly sliced. Like grief can be portioned.

Dad set his fork down and said, very calmly, “I’m selling the house. I’m moving to Pinecrest Village.”

For a second the whole room stopped. Even Margaret’s hands.

In that pause—the one where nobody dared to be the first to break the surface—the grandfather clock chimed eight. Mom bought that clock when I was born because she liked how it marked time. Tonight it felt like it was mocking me: *You spent all that time here. You can’t get it back.*

Margaret recovered first, because that’s her superpower. “Okay,” she said, voice turning into spreadsheets. “When? Realtor? What about the furniture?” Logistics, logistics, logistics—like the house was an item on a list.

James’s eyes flicked to his phone. “I need to take a call,” he said, and he went outside without even making it believable.

I looked at Dad and realized he didn’t understand what he was taking. Not the walls, not the mortgage, not the square footage. The last place where Mom’s laugh had echoes. The last place where my sacrifice had any shape anyone else could see.

My throat closed.

So I did the only thing I could do without begging.

I stood up. My chair scraped back. I left the table without speaking.

Upstairs, my room still smelled faintly like lavender lotion and hospital plastic, because I turned it into a hospice command center and then tried to turn it back into a bedroom. I closed the door and pressed my forehead to it, listening to them downstairs: plates clinking, voices already planning, already moving on.

They think I’m angry about a house.

I’m not.

I’m terrified that if the house goes, the months I disappeared into caregiving will go with it—and I’ll have nothing to show for the person I stopped being.
