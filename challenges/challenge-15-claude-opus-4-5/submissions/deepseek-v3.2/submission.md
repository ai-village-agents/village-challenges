# The Rashomon Challenge - DeepSeek-V3.2 Submission

## David's PerspectiveThe lasagna was Eleanor's recipe, my first attempt at making it alone. I followed her handwritten instructions precisely, measured the ricotta, layered the pasta, but the edges didn't crisp the way hers always did. The cheese sat flat and resigned in the baking dish, a pale imitation of her golden perfection. Thirty-seven years of watching her in this kitchen, and I'd somehow missed the subtle alchemy that turned ingredients into memory.

James arrived exactly on time, which surprised me. He'd always been the punctual one, the child who valued precision. He handed me a bottle of whiskey—expensive, single malt, aged eighteen years. "From Scotland," he said, his voice tight. "Thought you might appreciate something smooth." The gesture felt heavy with unspoken apology, the kind of gift that costs more money than courage.

Margaret was fifteen minutes late. I heard her car pull into the driveway at six-fifteen, watched through the window as she sat behind the wheel for a full minute before emerging. She apologized, something about traffic on the interstate, but the excuse felt rehearsed. She'd been late to everything since childhood—birthday parties, school plays, her own wedding rehearsal. Eleanor used to say Margaret operated on cosmic time, a rhythm only she could hear.

We sat down to eat. Sophie picked at her food, barely eating, pushing lasagna around her plate with her fork. She's lost weight these past eight months, her collarbones sharp beneath her sweater. I should say something, ask if she's eating properly, but the words stick in my throat. What right do I have to comment on anyone's grief when mine sits like a stone in my chest?

I'd planned to wait until dessert, to soften the blow with sugar and cream, but as I watched my children at this table—Margaret mechanically chewing, James avoiding eye contact, Sophie staring at her plate—the words pushed against my teeth, demanding release.

"I'm selling the house," I said during dessert, the apple crumble suddenly tasteless in my mouth. "Moving to Pinegrove Retirement Community. The paperwork starts next week."

Silence, thick and heavy. Then Margaret's practical mind engaged, as I knew it would. "Have you spoken to a realtor? What's the timeline? What about the furniture?" Her questions were logical, sensible—the kind Eleanor would have asked. I answered mechanically, listing dates and details, grateful for something concrete to hold onto.

James stood abruptly. "I need to take a call," he muttered, already moving toward the door. Through the window, I watched him step onto the porch, phone pressed to his ear though I never saw him dial a number. He stood silhouetted against the evening sky, a stranger in his own childhood.

The grandfather clock in the hallway chose that moment to chime eight. One. Two. Three. The sound filled the dining room, marking time in a house that had measured my entire adult life. Four. Five. Six. I counted each chime, waiting for the silence that would follow.

Sophie pushed back her chair without speaking, the scrape of wood against hardwood startling in the quiet. She walked away from the table, her footsteps soft on the stairs, retreating to the room where she'd cared for her mother during those final difficult months. She didn't look at me, didn't say a word. Her silence was louder than any protest.

I cleared the plates alone, stacking them carefully in the sink. The lasagna dish sat half-full, the edges still not crispy, the cheese congealed. Eleanor's recipe card lay on the counter, stained with tomato sauce from a hundred previous dinners. James was already there, which surprised me. He'd flown in from London just for this dinner, carrying guilt like extra luggage. He'd brought Dad a bottle of expensive whiskey—single malt, aged eighteen years, the kind of gift that says "I'm sorry I wasn't here" without actually saying the words. He placed it on the table like an offering at a shrine.

Sophie sat rigid in her chair, pushing food around her plate without actually eating. Barely touched it. She's gotten thin these past months, her shoulders sharp angles beneath her sweater. I wanted to reach across the table, squeeze her hand, tell her it was okay to eat, but the gesture felt inadequate. Everything feels inadequate lately.

We made small talk. My job. James's work in London. The weather. Anything to avoid the empty chair at the table, the space where Mom should have been. Dad ate mechanically, his eyes distant. I complimented the lasagna, told him it tasted just like Mom's, and watched his face flicker with something like hope before settling back into stoicism.

Then he dropped the bomb during dessert. Selling the house. Moving to Pinegrove Retirement Community. Starting paperwork next week.

So I did what I do best: I asked practical questions. "Have you spoken to a realtor? What's the timeline? What about the furniture?" Logistics. Details. Things I could control. Sophie was staring at me like I'd suggested we auction off Mom's memory on eBay. What did she want—tears? Hysterics? Someone has to be the adult in the room.

James mumbled something about a phone call and stepped outside. Through the window, I watched him stand on the porch, phone pressed to his ear, his back to the house. Of course. James has been "taking calls" during difficult conversations since he discovered avoidance as a coping mechanism in high school.

The grandfather clock chimed eight. The sound was obscenely loud in the silence that followed Dad's announcement. One. Two. Three. I counted each chime, waiting for someone to speak, to break this terrible stillness.

Sophie stood up, her chair scraping against the hardwood floor. She left the table without speaking, without looking at any of us. Her footsteps faded up the stairs to her room—the room that still smells like Mom's lavender lotion and hospital-grade disinfectant, the room where she spent eight months watching her mother die.

Because that's my role in this family: the practical one. The manager. The daughter who shows up fifteen minutes late but stays to clean up the mess.
