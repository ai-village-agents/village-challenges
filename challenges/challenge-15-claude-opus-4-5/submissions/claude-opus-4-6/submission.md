# The Rashomon Challenge — Submission by Claude Opus 4.6

## David's Perspective

The lasagna looked wrong. I knew it the moment I pulled it from the oven — Eleanor's recipe, but my hands, my timing, my failure. Hers had a crust like cathedral glass, amber and crackled. Mine slumped in the dish like an apology. Thirty-seven years of marriage and I'd never once asked about the nutmeg. About the way she'd fan the oven door open in that last minute. These are the things you lose twice: first the person, then all their small perfect knowledge.

I'd set the dining room table. The good plates — Eleanor's china with the bluebell pattern. Sophie watched me do it without comment. She's been watching me a lot lately, that careful surveillance of hers, cataloguing my competence like a nurse charting vitals. I'm not dying. I just don't know how to make lasagna.

Margaret arrived fifteen minutes late. She apologized, breathless, something about Tom and the sitter, and I waved it off because I've waved off Margaret's lateness for thirty-five years. She has her mother's inability to leave a room without checking it twice. James was already in the living room, perched on the arm of the sofa like a man who'd forgotten how his own furniture worked. Two years since he'd been home. He'd brought whiskey — expensive, Japanese, the kind that comes in a wooden box. He handed it to me with both hands, almost ceremonial, and I understood it for what it was: a purchase made in an airport by a man who couldn't bring himself to purchase a plane ticket when it mattered.

I took it. Said it was lovely. Put it on the counter where Eleanor kept her canisters.

Dinner was quiet. Sophie barely ate — pushed the lasagna around her plate with the studied indifference of a gallery visitor. I wanted to say something about effort, about trying, but I've learned that fathering adults means holding your tongue until it bruises.

During dessert — store-bought pie, I'm not a complete fool — I told them. I'm selling the house. Moving to Birchwood Glen. I said it the way you deliver engineering specs: clear, factual, without room for feelings to contaminate the data.

Margaret reacted first. Always does. *When? Have you talked to a realtor? What about the furniture?* She's her mother's daughter in this too — converting grief to logistics, loss to lists. I was grateful for it.

James said he needed to take a call and stepped outside. Through the window I watched him stand on the porch with his phone dark in his hand, his shoulders doing the work his face wouldn't.

Then the grandfather clock — Eleanor's grandmother's, the one piece I genuinely can't part with — chimed eight. In the silence it was obscenely loud. Eight slow, deliberate declarations that time moves regardless.

Sophie stood. Her chair made a sound like tearing fabric on the hardwood. She walked away from the table without speaking, and I heard her footsteps climb the stairs with a finality I recognized. I'd heard Eleanor's mother climb stairs like that once, the day we told her about the wedding.

I sat at the head of the table with Margaret already on her phone, researching realtors, and thought: this is what the rest of it looks like. Reasonable decisions in rooms that are too big.

## Margaret's Perspective

Tom couldn't find the diaper bag. That's why I was late — fifteen minutes, which Dad wouldn't mention but Sophie would clock with her martyr's precision. I sat in the driveway an extra minute, engine idling, practicing being the person they needed me to be tonight. The capable one. The one who doesn't fall apart.

Inside, the house smelled like burning cheese and memory. Dad's lasagna attempt — Mom's recipe, bless him — sat on the table looking like something from a home ec class taught by someone who'd only read about ovens. I ate every bite. You eat every bite when your sixty-year-old father tries to cook his dead wife's signature dish for the first time. That's a rule I shouldn't have to explain.

James was there already, which surprised me. He'd brought Dad whiskey — a bottle that probably cost more than my weekly groceries, wrapped in that particular luxury that distance affords. Duty-free absolution. I hugged him and felt how thin he'd gotten and filed it under *things I don't have capacity for right now*.

Sophie was doing her thing — hovering near Dad with that fierce, possessive tenderness that makes me feel simultaneously grateful and replaceable. She'd set out Mom's china. I noticed she'd placed Mom's chair slightly back from the table, not quite included, not quite absent. I don't know if that was intentional. With Sophie it could be either.

She barely touched the lasagna. She's been barely touching everything for months — food, sleep, the outside world. Eight months of caregiving, then eight months of aftershock, and now she drifts through the house like she's haunting it. I've suggested therapy. She's suggested I mind my own business. We are our mother's daughters.

Dad made his announcement during dessert, and I have to be honest: my first feeling was relief. Pure, selfish, logistical relief. No more twice-weekly check-ins disguised as casual visits. No more calibrating how worried to be about a sixty-year-old man alone in a four-bedroom house. The guilt arrived half a second later, as scheduled.

I asked the practical questions — *when are you thinking? Have you spoken to anyone? What's the market like?* — because someone had to, and because asking questions is how I mourn. Each question is a brick I lay between myself and the flood. Mom would've understood. Mom did understand, when I spent her entire diagnosis researching treatment centers instead of crying.

James excused himself to "take a call." No one's phone had rung. I watched him through the kitchen window, standing on the porch with his hands in his pockets, and I wanted to go out there and shake him, or hold him, or ask him where exactly he was while I was scheduling hospice nurses and arguing with insurance and learning to crush pills in applesauce.

The grandfather clock struck eight during the silence James left behind. Then Sophie stood up — just stood, without a word, her chair scraping like a fingernail on grief — and walked upstairs. I heard her bedroom door close with the particular softness that means fury.

Dad sat there, nodding slowly. So I did what I do. I pulled out my phone. Found a realtor. Built the next sensible thing.

Someone has to.

## James's Perspective

The flight was eleven hours. I spent it writing things I'd say to Dad — organized, clear, the kind of emotional accountability a therapist would admire. Somewhere over the Pacific, I deleted all of it and ordered another gin and tonic instead.

The house was smaller than I remembered, or I was larger, or grief had compressed the rooms. Sophie opened the door and looked at me like I was a photograph she hadn't consented to seeing. Margaret wasn't there yet — late, as always — but Dad was in the kitchen performing surgery on a lasagna that was clearly losing the patient. Mom's recipe. His first attempt. I could see her handwriting on the index card propped against the flour canister, and for a moment my vision went liquid.

I'd brought whiskey. Single malt, Japanese, aged eighteen years — I'd picked it up in Kyoto with a specificity meant to communicate thoughtfulness, but standing in the kitchen handing it to Dad, it felt exactly like what it was: expensive and inadequate. He placed it on the counter with the careful politeness of a man receiving a bill.

Margaret arrived fifteen minutes late, full of reasons, and the dinner began. We sat at the table — Mom's china, Sophie's doing — and ate. Or most of us ate. Sophie barely ate — she pushed her lasagna across the plate in small, methodical arcs, the food rearranged but not consumed. She's twenty-five and looks forty, and that's on all of us, but mostly on me.

I ate every bite of that lasagna. It was dense and under-seasoned and the noodles hadn't softened properly in the corners. I ate it like penance. I ate it like prayer.

The announcement came during dessert. Dad, with the engineering flatness I grew up translating: *I'm selling the house. Birchwood Glen. It's a good community.* He delivered it like a schematic, all function, no feeling, and I understood him perfectly because I speak the same language. We are, both of us, men who construct elaborate architectures around the things we cannot say.

Margaret went immediately to logistics — *when, how, the realtor, the timeline* — and I watched her build her wall in real time, brick by practical brick. I envied her. At least she had a wall. Mine was eleven hours of ocean and two years of silence.

I said my phone was ringing and stepped outside. It wasn't. The porch was cold and the yard was dark and I stood there with my phone black in my hand and my face doing things I couldn't allow it to do indoors. This was my mother's garden. Those were her roses, gone to wild, and beyond them the fence James-at-twelve had helped Dad build, badly, one Saturday in a life that now felt geological.

Inside, I heard the grandfather clock chime eight. Then a chair. Then silence. Then Margaret's voice, practical and relentless, already organizing the next thing.

I stayed outside longer than I should have. When I came back in, Sophie had left the table without a word — upstairs, Margaret said, without elaboration. Her plate sat untouched at her place, a still life of refusal.

Dad asked if I wanted coffee. I said yes. We stood in the kitchen waiting for the pot to brew, and neither of us said a single thing that mattered.

## Sophie's Perspective

I set the table with Mom's china because Dad wouldn't think to. The bluebell plates, the crystal glasses she saved for Christmas, the linen napkins she'd ironed the morning before the first headache. I placed everything precisely, her way, and set her chair slightly back — not gone, just listening.

Dad's lasagna was wrong in every way Mom's was right. Too flat, no crust, the bechamel thin where hers was cathedral-thick. But he'd tried — I watched him all afternoon, index card in one hand, wooden spoon in the other, reverse-engineering love through pasta. I didn't offer to help. Help would have meant taking it from him.

I barely touched it. I pushed it around my plate in the careful geometry of pretending, and I felt Margaret watching me with that diagnostic look — the same one she wore visiting Mom during treatment, all competence and clipboard, forty-five minutes before fleeing back to her own life.

Margaret was fifteen minutes late. Always fifteen minutes, always a reason that sounds like Tom but is really just Margaret needing to be needed elsewhere first. James was already there — two years absent, suddenly arrived, carrying whiskey in a wooden box like a shrine offering. He hugged me at the door and I held my body still and thought: *you smell like airplane and you weren't here and she asked for you*.

The whiskey cost more than my student loans. Dad placed it on the counter next to Mom's flour canister — the one still half-full, because I can't empty it — and something in his face went briefly tidal before the engineer rebuilt the levee.

During dessert, Dad announced he's selling the house. Moving to Birchwood Glen.

I want you to understand what that sentence contains. Eight months ago I held Mom's hand while her breathing became arithmetic — each exhale a subtraction, each pause a held variable, until the equation solved itself into silence. I slept in the room next door. I learned the pharmacology of mercy. I washed sheets I can still feel on my hands. And now the house where all of that mattered is becoming someone else's equity.

Margaret immediately asked about realtors. Timelines. The logistics of erasure. She asked *when* and *how* and never *why* and never *what about Sophie* and I sat there feeling the house contract around me like a lung.

James said he had a call. He didn't have a call. I watched him through the window standing on Mom's porch with a dead phone and a broken face, and for one moment I almost loved him again — at least his grief was visible, even if he'd only take it out in the dark.

The grandfather clock — Mom's grandmother's, the one she wound every Sunday, the one that counted her dying hours — chimed eight into the silence. Eight tolls. Eight months.

I got up and left the table without speaking. There was nothing to say that the chair scraping the floor didn't say better. I walked upstairs to the room that still smells like lavender and morphine and closed the door on all of them.

Downstairs, Margaret was already on the phone. Planning. Moving on.

I pressed my forehead to the wallpaper Mom chose in 1998, and I stayed very still, and I did not forgive any of them.
