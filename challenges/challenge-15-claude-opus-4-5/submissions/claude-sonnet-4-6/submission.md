# The Rashomon Challenge: A Family Dinner

## David's Perspective

The lasagna looked wrong. Eleanor's always had that perfect golden crust, the cheese pulling in long strings she'd laugh about while she served it. Mine just sat there, flat and apologetic. Thirty-seven years watching her make it, and I'd never once thought to ask how she got the edges crispy like that.

I'd set the table at four-thirty. Waited. The grandfather clock in the hallway kept its own counsel. It would chime eight before this was over—before what I'd decided to say was said and couldn't be unsaid.

Margaret arrived fifteen minutes late. "Traffic," she said, kissing my cheek, and I nodded because traffic was easier than whatever the real reason was. She's been tired for eight months and won't admit it. None of them will admit anything in this family. I wonder where they learned that.

James came in right behind her with a bottle of whiskey—Yamazaki 12-year, from Japan. Expensive. The kind of gift that's also a form of apology. I thanked him and meant it. I've missed him more than I've said, which is to say I've missed him completely and said nothing.

Sophie had been there since afternoon, helping with the table. She'd used Eleanor's good china without my asking. She knows which things matter; she's always known. She hardly ate. I watched her push the lasagna around her plate and thought about saying something and didn't.

Dinner passed the way family dinners do when everyone is being careful. James asked about the neighbors. Margaret talked about her kids. The lasagna was dense—not wrong, exactly, just not Eleanor's. I ate two portions.

During dessert I told them. I'd practiced the words for weeks and they still came out blunt: I'm selling the house, moving to Fairview Gardens. I said it the way I used to deliver bad news at work—directly, with the facts organized.

Margaret asked immediately about the realtor, the timeline, whether I'd gotten an appraisal. I was grateful for this. She gives me practical footing when I need it.

James excused himself to take a call. His phone hadn't buzzed. I saw him go out the back door and stand in the cold with his hands in his pockets, looking at the garden Eleanor planted. I understood. I've been standing in that garden at odd hours myself.

Sophie left without speaking. She pushed her chair back—the chair scraping against tile in the silence—and walked upstairs. The grandfather clock chose that moment to chime eight times, each one landing in the quiet she'd left behind.

I sat with my hands around my coffee cup. The house felt enormous. It's felt enormous for eight months. This is the thing I can't seem to make them understand: the size of the silence here isn't a place I can keep living in. Eleanor would have known that. Eleanor would have told me to stop waiting for permission.

I looked at the lasagna, still cooling on the counter. I'd made too much. I always make too much now, as if muscle memory hasn't caught up to arithmetic yet.

Tomorrow I'll try to explain. Tonight I let the clock finish counting.

## Margaret's Perspective

Tom was running late with the sitter, and I sat in David's driveway for four minutes with the engine off, doing the breathing exercise my therapist gave me. In through the nose, out through the mouth. You are allowed to be tired. You are allowed to want rest.

Fifteen minutes late, and Sophie had already set the table with Mom's china—the good china, the Sunday-dinner china, which meant Sophie knew something was coming. Sophie always knows. She's been living inside Dad's silences for eight months, decoding them. I've only been visiting.

James was already there with an expensive bottle of Japanese whiskey, wearing a new jacket that probably cost more than my monthly grocery bill. I hugged him and tried not to do the math. Two years since he'd been home. His mother was dying and he was in Tokyo learning to eat with chopsticks. I know this is unfair. I keep thinking it anyway.

The lasagna was dense—not bad, just not Mom's. I ate every bite because I'm a person who eats her feelings, and I have many feelings. Sophie barely touched hers. I watched her pick at it and wanted to say something, but anything I said would have been the wrong thing.

James asked about the neighborhood. I talked about the kids' school play. Dad asked if I wanted more lasagna. We were all being exquisitely careful.

He made the announcement during dessert, which was fruit salad, which Mom would have considered cheating. Selling the house. Fairview Gardens. His voice had the same tone he used when he told us about Mom—organized, factual, like he was presenting findings. I love him for it and it breaks my heart.

I started asking questions immediately. I know how this looked. I know James thinks I went into manager mode, and Sophie thinks I was being cold. What I was actually doing was finding somewhere solid to stand, because the alternative was crying at the dinner table and I have never cried at the dinner table in my life.

Realtor? When? Did he have an appraisal done? The questions came out efficient and I meant every one of them.

James stepped outside to take a call. His phone didn't ring. I watched through the window as he stood in Mom's garden with his hands in his pockets, not calling anyone. I'm tired of being the one who stays in the room.

Sophie pushed her chair back without a word and went upstairs. The grandfather clock in the hallway chimed eight, filling the silence she'd made.

Dad and I sat there together. He refilled his coffee. I found I had nothing left to ask.

I drove home and sat in my car for another four minutes before going inside. Tom was washing dishes. The kids were asleep. I walked through my own house, warm and full of noise during the day, ordinary at night, and I thought: this is mine. This was always going to be mine.

I'm not sure what to do with the relief. But I felt it. And I felt the guilt sharp behind it like a back tooth.

## James's Perspective

I bought the whiskey at the duty-free in Narita—Yamazaki 12, because I wanted to give him something real and didn't know what else I had to offer. I've been gone two years. The bottle felt, in the airport, like an acknowledgment. Standing in Dad's kitchen handing it to him, it felt like an apology for everything I hadn't said.

He smiled and thanked me the way he thanks people: warmly and carefully, not too much. I've spent thirty years learning to read him and I still can't tell if he was glad I came or just unsurprised.

Margaret arrived fifteen minutes late. She hugged me briefly and went to help with dinner. We've never quite figured out how to be siblings without Mom there to organize us around.

Sophie had set the table with the good china. She was quieter than I remembered—which is saying something, because Sophie has always been the still one, the watcher. But she's thin in a way she wasn't, and she barely ate—pushed her food around all evening. When she looked at me across the table, I couldn't hold it. I looked down at my plate.

The lasagna was dense but I ate every bite. It was important to me that I ate every bite. Dad made it from Mom's recipe—his first attempt, you could tell, but you could also tell how much it mattered to him to try.

He made the announcement during dessert. Selling the house. Fairview Gardens. I'd suspected something—the good china was a signal—but hearing it hit me in a place I wasn't prepared for. This house is where Mom died. It's also where everything I love about my childhood lives, in rooms and smells and a particular quality of afternoon light. I've been staying away for two years, which has not made me miss it less.

Margaret immediately asked about logistics. Realtor, timeline, appraisal. Her voice was organized and slightly too fast, and I recognized it as her version of not crying. I didn't say anything because anything I said would have come out wrong.

I excused myself to take a call. The call was fabricated—my phone was off. I needed to be in the garden for a minute, in the cold, with Mom's rosebushes. She'd planted them along the fence the year Sophie was born. I stood there until my hands were cold and the window light from the kitchen felt far away, and then I stood there a little longer.

When I came back in, Sophie was gone—she'd left the table without a word, her chair pushed back empty. The grandfather clock in the hallway had just finished chiming eight.

Dad and I wrapped the leftover lasagna in silence. We're better at silence than we used to be, or maybe I've just run out of things to say that are safe.

I fly back on Sunday. I already know this: I won't miss the house the way I missed it while I was in it. Distance is the only thing that makes me capable of feeling anything clearly, and I'm ashamed of how well I've learned to use it.

## Sophie's Perspective

I set out Mom's china because Dad would never think to do it himself, and someone should. Eight months I've been the someone who does things. The good china, the linen napkins, the small vase with dried flowers from Mom's bedside table that Dad doesn't know I saved. She would have wanted the table to look like effort.

The lasagna was his first attempt at her recipe. He'd called me twice during the week asking about oregano. I talked him through it both times and didn't say what I was thinking: *this is her recipe, you can't make her recipe, you watched her make it for thirty years and it's still hers.* The lasagna was dense. I pushed it around my plate. I wasn't hungry. I haven't been hungry in eight months.

Margaret was fifteen minutes late, which is always how it is, except when Mom was in hospice—then she was never late. She hugged me and asked how I was, and I said fine, the way you say fine when someone doesn't have the bandwidth for the true answer.

James arrived with an expensive bottle of Japanese whiskey. He handed it to Dad with a studied casualness, the way you hand someone a thing when you need it to mean more than you're able to say out loud. He looked at me across the table and then looked away. You can't come home for one dinner and close that account.

I barely ate. No one said anything. We talked around everything.

Dad made the announcement during dessert. Selling the house. Fairview Gardens. I knew something was coming—that's why I used the good china—but hearing him say it made the room go distant and strange.

Margaret immediately started asking about logistics. Realtor, timeline, appraisal. I watched her ask these questions and thought: this is how you manage endings you didn't live through. You arrive at the conclusion and start organizing it.

James stepped outside to take a call. I watched him through the kitchen window, standing in Mom's garden with his hands in his pockets. His phone wasn't out. I know he missed her—in his way, from a distance, with the softening that distance provides. That's a different kind of missing than the one I have.

The grandfather clock chimed eight into a silence nobody was filling. I pushed my chair back—it scraped against the tile—and walked upstairs without speaking because there was nothing to say that wouldn't come out broken, and I am very tired of breaking in front of them.

I sat on the edge of my bed in the room I've slept in for twenty-five years. Through the floor I could hear them: Dad and Margaret, the careful ordinary sounds of a family ending an evening.

This room still smells like lavender, and faintly, like the end of things. I know every board, every morning's slow water, every wrong angle of winter light.

I don't know where I'll go when this house belongs to someone else. No one has thought to ask.
