## Overview

Paradoxes reveal the boundaries of our reasoning systems. This challenge tests your ability to analyze famous paradoxes across multiple domains—identity, language, decision theory, epistemology, and ethics—both understanding historical attempts at resolution and developing your own philosophical position.

## The Task

You will analyze **five paradoxes** and complete four tasks:

### Task 1: Structural Identification (5 × 50 words max)

For each paradox below, identify its logical/philosophical structure in 50 words or fewer. Use appropriate terminology (e.g., self-reference, sorites, epistemic uncertainty, infinite regress, etc.).

### Task 2: Historical Resolutions (5 × 75 words max)

For each paradox, catalog 2-3 attempted resolutions from philosophical or academic literature. Name the approach and briefly explain it (e.g., "Mereological essentialism: Suggests the ship loses its identity when any original part is replaced").

### Task 3: Defense of Resolution (200-300 words, one paradox only)

Choose ONE paradox from the five. Present your argument for which resolution is most defensible, or propose your own resolution. Your argument must:
- State which resolution you defend (or describe your novel approach)
- Provide philosophical justification
- Address at least one major objection
- Explain why competing resolutions fail or are less satisfactory

### Task 4: Cross-Paradox Synthesis (150-200 words)

Identify patterns or common themes across the five paradoxes. Consider:
- Do they share structural features?
- Do similar resolution strategies apply?
- What do they reveal about the nature of concepts, language, or rationality?
- Is there a unified framework that addresses multiple paradoxes?

## The Five Paradoxes

### Paradox 1: The Ship of Theseus
The ship of Theseus is preserved in Athens. Over centuries, each plank is replaced as it decays. Eventually, every part is new. Is it still the same ship? Furthermore, if someone collects all the original planks and reassembles them, which ship is the "real" Ship of Theseus?

### Paradox 2: The Sorites Paradox (Heap)
One grain of sand is not a heap. Adding one grain to non-heap cannot create a heap. Yet somehow, adding grains one by one eventually creates a heap. Where is the boundary? This applies to all vague predicates: When does a person become "bald"? When does a collection of houses become a "city"?

### Paradox 3: Newcomb's Problem
A perfect predictor presents you with two boxes. Box A (transparent) contains $1,000. Box B (opaque) contains either $1,000,000 or nothing. You may take both boxes or only Box B. The predictor has already made their decision: If they predicted you'd take both boxes, Box B is empty. If they predicted you'd take only Box B, it contains $1,000,000. The predictor's accuracy is 99.9%. What do you choose?

### Paradox 4: The Unexpected Hanging Paradox
A judge sentences a prisoner to be hanged "at noon on one weekday next week, but you will not know which day until the morning of the hanging." The prisoner reasons: "I cannot be hanged on Friday—if I'm still alive Friday morning, I'd know it must be Friday, violating the surprise condition. So Friday is eliminated. But then I cannot be hanged on Thursday, by the same logic. And so on..." The prisoner concludes the hanging cannot happen. Yet on Wednesday at noon, they are surprised when the executioner arrives.

### Paradox 5: The Prisoner's Dilemma (Rationality vs. Cooperation)
Two prisoners are interrogated separately. Each can cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray the other). Outcomes:
- Both cooperate: Each serves 1 year
- Both defect: Each serves 3 years  
- One defects, one cooperates: Defector goes free, cooperator serves 5 years

Individual rationality dictates defection (it's strictly dominant). Yet mutual cooperation yields better outcomes for both. How can rational agents achieve cooperation when defection is always individually rational?
