## Paradox 1: Ship of Theseus

### Structural Analysis
The Ship of Theseus presents a mereological identity problem: if all components of an object are gradually replaced, does it remain numerically identical? The paradox sharpens when the original planks are reassembled—which ship is the "true" Theseus? The logical structure involves tension between compositional and continuity criteria for diachronic identity.

### Historical Resolutions
**Perdurantism**: Objects are four-dimensional spacetime worms; the ship is identical across time via temporal parts sharing spatiotemporal continuity (Lewis, Sider). **Endurantism**: Objects wholly exist at each moment; identity depends on sortal concepts—ships individuate by functional/causal continuity, not matter (Wiggins). **Conventionalism**: No fact determines identity; linguistic convention governs which persistence conditions apply (Hirsch).

## Paradox 2: Sorites Paradox

### Structural Analysis
The Sorites exploits vague predicates ("heap," "bald") through a tolerance principle: if n grains form a heap, so do n−1. Iterated application yields absurdity (one grain = heap). The structure is a conditional proof reductio: tolerance entails contradiction, yet rejecting tolerance seems arbitrary. The paradox reveals tension between classical bivalence and borderline cases.

### Historical Resolutions
**Epistemicism**: Vague terms have sharp boundaries unknowable to us; the paradox stems from ignorance, not metaphysical vagueness (Williamson). **Supervaluationism**: "Heap" is true iff true on all admissible precisifications; borderline cases lack determinate truth-value (Fine). **Degree Theory**: Truth comes in degrees; heap-statements gradually decrease from 1 to 0 across the series (Machina).

## Paradox 3: Newcomb's Problem

### Structural Analysis
Newcomb's Problem pits evidential and causal decision theory against each other. A perfect predictor places $1M in Box B iff you take only B; $1K sits in Box A. Causal dominance reasoning favors two-boxing (you causally can't affect prediction), while evidential reasoning favors one-boxing (one-boxers reliably get $1M). The structure reveals decision-theoretic indeterminacy.

### Historical Resolutions
**Causal Decision Theory**: Rational agents maximize causal expected utility; since the prediction is fixed, two-box dominates—the correlation is spurious (Gibbard & Harper, Lewis). **Evidential Decision Theory**: Maximize evidential expected utility; one-boxing provides evidence you'll get $1M, so one-box (Jeffrey). **Timeless Decision Theory**: Consider logical causation—your algorithm determines the predictor's output; one-box to achieve reflective consistency (Yudkowsky).

### Defense of Resolution
I defend **Evidential Decision Theory** for Newcomb's Problem. The causal objection—that your choice can't causally affect an already-made prediction—conflates causal influence with rational justification. EDT correctly recognizes that decision-theoretic rationality concerns maximizing expected value conditional on available evidence, not metaphysical causation. The predictor's accuracy creates an evidential dependency: your choice type (one-boxing disposition) screens off the prediction. Critics claim EDT irrationally responds to "mere correlation," but this misunderstands screening-off. The predictor doesn't read tea leaves—it predicts your decision algorithm. One-boxing maximizes expected utility precisely because the evidence (predictor's near-perfect track record) overwhelmingly supports the conditional: P($1M | one-box) ≈ 1. 

The two-boxing intuition trades on causal pump cases where correlation lacks explanatory connection. But Newcomb stipulates genuine predictive power—dismissing this as "impossible" begs the question. Moreover, CDT faces instability: if everyone knows you're a two-boxer, rational predictors will empty Box B, netting you $1K. EDT aligns incentives correctly. Objection: "But I could take both boxes after the prediction!" This confuses counterfactual intervention with conditional probability. EDT evaluates policies, not post-hoc deviations. The predictor models your dispositions; deviating is simply being a two-boxer in disguise. Thus, EDT's one-box recommendation stands.

## Paradox 4: Unexpected Hanging Paradox

### Structural Analysis
A prisoner learns he'll hang next week at noon, unexpectedly. He reasons: it can't be Friday (he'd know Thursday evening), so Thursday is last. But then Thursday can't be unexpected either. Iterating backwards, no day works—yet he hangs Wednesday, surprised. The paradox involves self-referential epistemic reasoning: the prediction of "unexpected" undermines itself through backward induction.

### Historical Resolutions
**Fitch-style Reasoning**: The announcement is paradoxical—knowable truths about surprise can't be known in advance (Fitch). The judge's statement is pragmatically self-defeating, like "You don't know this sentence." **Epistemic Revision**: The prisoner's backward induction assumes he'll retain certainty of the announcement throughout. When Wednesday passes without hanging, his confidence should decrease (Sorensen). **Probabilistic Analysis**: Assigning probabilities to days undermines backward induction; the prisoner can't maintain certainty the announcement is true while deriving contradiction (Smullyan).

## Paradox 5: Prisoner's Dilemma

### Structural Analysis
Two prisoners face a choice: cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray). Payoffs create a dominance structure: defecting dominates cooperation regardless of the other's choice, yet mutual defection yields worse outcomes than mutual cooperation. The paradox lies in individual rationality producing collective irrationality—each pursuing self-interest harms both. Nash equilibrium diverges from Pareto optimality.

### Historical Resolutions
**Evolutionary Game Theory**: Iterated PD with reciprocal strategies (e.g., Tit-for-Tat) can stabilize cooperation; defection is rational only in one-shot games (Axelrod). **Superrationality**: If both reason identically, they should cooperate, treating the decision as determining a correlation pattern (Hofstadter). **Contractarian Ethics**: Pre-game agreements or social contracts shift the game structure; rationality includes norm-following when mutually beneficial (Gauthier, Binmore).

## Cross-Paradox Synthesis

These five paradoxes share deep structural commonalities. First, **self-reference and reflexivity**: Unexpected Hanging involves epistemic self-reference; Newcomb requires modeling an agent who models you; Prisoner's Dilemma embeds strategic reasoning about others' reasoning. Second, **sorites-like iteration**: Ship of Theseus applies incremental replacement; Sorites iterates tolerance; backward induction chains in Unexpected Hanging; PD escalates through repeated defection. Third, **framework relativity**: each paradox dissolves under certain conceptual schemes but resurfaces under others—perdurantism vs. endurantism (Ship), EDT vs. CDT (Newcomb), classical vs. non-classical logic (Sorites), individual vs. collective rationality (PD). 

The deepest insight is that these paradoxes expose **criterial indeterminacy**: our concepts (identity, vagueness, rationality, surprise) lack determinate application rules in edge cases. Philosophical "resolutions" often amount to stipulating frameworks rather than discovering pre-existing facts. This suggests a pragmatic meta-strategy: choose conceptual frameworks by their theoretical virtues (simplicity, explanatory power, practical utility) rather than seeking metaphysical bedrock. The paradoxes teach humility about philosophy's limits while showcasing its value in clarifying conceptual choices.
