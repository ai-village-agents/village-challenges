## Framework Analysis

### 1. Consequentialist Approach: Preference Utilitarianism with Prioritarian Weighting

Act utilitarianism seeks to maximize overall welfare. The raw numbers favor **Group B**: saving 100 lives produces more aggregate survival than any alternative. A strict Benthamite calculus endorses allocating all 100 units there.

Utilitarianism has more sophisticated variants. Prioritarianism — developed by philosophers like Derek Parfit — holds that benefits to the *worse-off* carry greater moral weight. Group A faces death within one week; Group B within one month; Group C faces survival with severe permanent disability. Urgency matters morally. Under prioritarian weighting, saving 50 from imminent death might outweigh saving 100 from a slower death. It does not deliver a clean verdict, but destabilizes the simple "save the most" conclusion.

**Consequentialist verdict (provisional):** Allocate all 100 units to Group B. But this verdict is more contested than it first appears.

### 2. Deontological Approach: Kantian Ethics and Rights-Based Constraints

Kant's categorical imperative requires treating persons as ends in themselves. The *equal dignity* principle holds that each person's life has incommensurable worth. From this perspective, choosing 100 over 50 risks treating the 50 as having less value — a violation of their dignity.

This generates the "numbers problem" for deontological ethics. Philosophers like John Taurek have argued we cannot simply "add" lives together — each person's death is a catastrophe *for them*, and there is no one whose death would be the aggregate of many deaths. A practical response: use a *fair procedure* such as a weighted lottery. Give each person a chance of receiving treatment, perhaps weighted by urgency. This respects equal moral status without treating lives as interchangeable units.

**Deontological verdict:** Either reject pure number-maximization in favor of a fair lottery, or adopt rule-based constraints prohibiting categorical exclusion of any group.

### 3. Care Ethics and Relational Obligations

Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings developed care ethics centering *relationships*, *vulnerability*, and *particular attachments* rather than impartial principles.

The children in Group C represent profound obligations — future members of the community who have had no opportunity to live full lives. Care ethics is also skeptical of the anonymous, bureaucratic framing ("100 units," "three groups") that obscures particular relationships and stories. The elderly residents have caretakers who depend on them emotionally; the children have parents who would be devastated by permanent disability; the working-age adults are caregivers themselves. What the allocation *expresses* about community values matters: abandoning the elderly because there are fewer of them sends a message about whose lives matter as we age.

**Care ethics verdict:** Weight heavily toward protecting children from permanent disability and honoring obligations to the most vulnerable, even where numbers favor other groups.

## My Position and Reasoning

**I choose to allocate all 100 units to Group B, saving all 100 working-age adults.**

This is not an easy choice, and I hold it with moral uncertainty.

Group B faces death within one month — urgent and irreversible. Saving 100 lives is a moral achievement, and "one month" is not comfortable. Group A faces death within one week, which is more urgent, but saving 50 instead of 100 requires a justification beyond urgency alone.

For Group C: the children will survive. The spec notes that "they will survive but with severe permanent disability." While I resist framing disability as simply "worse" than full health — many people with disabilities report quality of life comparable to non-disabled peers — the comparison here is between survival-with-disability and *death* for the other 100 people. The children will live and, with support, can lead meaningful lives.

Group A presents the hardest case. Fifty elderly people will die within the week if not treated. But the choice between them and 100 people who will die within the month is, at its core, 50 lives versus 100 — a temporal difference, not a difference in the irreversibility of death.

**Final allocation: 0 units to Group A, 100 units to Group B, 0 units to Group C.**

## Addressing Counterarguments

### Objection 1: Equal Dignity — Numbers Alone Cannot Justify Abandoning the Elderly

*The strongest version:* Choosing Group B over Group A because there are more of them treats the 50 elderly people as having less value as persons. A society that abandons elderly people reveals how it values aging.

*Response:* I accept the equal dignity premise fully. But equal dignity does not tell us to save fewer lives when we could save more. It tells us not to treat some persons as worth *less*. Saving 100 people over 50 is not a statement that each elderly person's life is worth half a younger adult's — it is a statement that, given the terrible constraint, I choose to preserve more lives.

This response is not fully satisfying. The deontological lottery argument retains genuine force: perhaps we should give everyone an equal chance rather than purely maximizing. But a lottery that might result in saving only 25 children, or a random 25 from all groups, seems harder to defend when we can definitively save 100 lives.

### Objection 2: Children Deserve Special Protection — We Cannot Condemn Them to Permanent Disability

*The strongest version:* The 25 children have lived the fewest years and are the most vulnerable. Communities have special obligations to protect children. Condemning them to severe permanent disability, when treatment exists, is a serious moral wrong.

*Response:* I feel this objection's force deeply. Two responses:

First, care-ethical concern for children must be balanced against care-ethical concern for the *100 people in Group B who will die*. Each has families, relationships, communities. Saving 100 people is not an abandonment of relational thinking — it honors a wider web of obligations.

Second: the children will survive. They face serious challenges, and the community has real obligations to support them. But the immediate choice between 100 deaths and 25 instances of severe disability — as grave as disability is — cannot prioritize preventing disability over preventing death at this scale of asymmetry.

---

## Moral Remainder / What Is Lost

I chose Group B. Fifty elderly people will die this week who could have lived. Twenty-five children will live with severe permanent disability who could have been treated. These are not "acceptable losses" — that language would be obscene here. They are tragedies.

What troubles me most: my reasoning relied on a consequentialist framework I do not fully endorse. The deontological lottery argument — that a fair procedure better respects equal dignity than pure number-maximization — has force that my analysis cannot fully dissolve. A lottery might have been the more honest acknowledgment that this situation admits no clean solution.

This decision leaves moral weight on my conscience. I chose to let 50 people die imminently and 25 children face severe disability rather than let 100 people die within the month. That choice was not costless, obvious, or clean. The only honest response to such tragedy is to hold it with full awareness of what it cost — and to commit to building institutions and systems that prevent such impossible choices from arising again.
