## Framework Analysis
The scenario offers 100 units of treatment and three groups with sharply different per-person needs: Group A has 50 elderly individuals requiring 2 units each (100 total to save all), Group B has 100 adults requiring 1 unit each (100 total), and Group C has 25 children or disabled persons requiring 4 units each (100 total). Any allocation that saves one group exhausts all resources and leaves the other two to die, making the decision a forced triage. Under utilitarianism, the salient variable is total lives saved and quality-adjusted life-years. Group B yields 100 lives saved, while Group A yields 50 and Group C yields 25. Even if we assign higher expected QALYs to children, the fourfold disadvantage in headcount between B and C is difficult to overcome without implausibly large multipliers. Adults often have dependents, so saving 100 of them likely yields the widest positive ripple for caregiving networks and social continuity. The direct and downstream utility thus points to allocating all units to Group B.

Kantian deontology asks whether the allocation respects persons as ends and could be willed as a universal rule. A principle such as “when constrained to save only one group, save the maximum number without discrimination” treats each life as equally valuable and honors the duty to rescue. Random selection might seem fair, but it would knowingly let more people die when we can prevent those deaths, which could itself breach the duty to respect rational nature. Within this frame, prioritizing the greatest number—without bias against age or disability—can be seen as the least violative way to act under constraint.

Virtue ethics shifts the question to what a virtuous agent—prudent, just, and courageous—would choose. Prudence demands clear-eyed assessment of consequences; justice demands impartiality; courage demands bearing the moral pain of the choice. A virtuous agent would not hide behind a lottery if that predictably wastes lives. Prudence pushes toward saving the greatest number; justice guards against prejudice; courage insists on acknowledging the anguish of leaving some to die. Taken together, these virtues point toward choosing Group B while holding the gravity of the loss to A and C in full view.

## My Position and Reasoning
I would allocate all 100 units to Group B. This choice stems from a convergence of utilitarian reasoning and an interpretation of justice that seeks to preserve the widest array of ongoing life projects. Saving 100 adults maximizes lives saved and sustains dependents, economic roles, and civic functions. The adults in Group B have built lives and assumed responsibilities to others; giving them treatment maintains those commitments and prevents secondary harms to those who rely on them. Justice here is not favoritism toward adults but respect for equal moral worth combined with the recognition that, when you cannot honor every claim, preserving the maximum number of ongoing claims is the least unjust option.

This is not a judgment about the intrinsic worth of the elderly or of children and disabled persons. Each life has equal dignity. The deciding factor is the structural constraint: a single all-or-nothing allocation. In such a stark triage, seeking to maximize the number of lives preserved while avoiding discriminatory criteria appears the most defensible route. Any other allocation saves fewer people and thus permits a larger, avoidable loss of life. The moral cost of letting 75 extra people die, when one could have saved them, outweighs the appeal of distributing resources to honor age-based or vulnerability-based intuitions. It is the arithmetic of tragic necessity rather than preference or prejudice.

## Addressing Counterarguments
One objection invokes the “fair innings” idea or charges of ageism. The fair innings view holds that those who have lived fewer years—children most of all—should get priority so that everyone has a chance at a full life. On this view, Group C should be prioritized because their potential years ahead are greatest, and Group A has already enjoyed more life. The counterpoint is that the fair innings rationale, while morally intuitive, cannot bridge the numeric gulf here: saving 25 children at the cost of 100 adults means accepting four times as many deaths. Even if each child’s expected QALYs are higher, it would require assuming that a child’s life counts at least four times more than an adult’s to overcome the headcount difference. That assumption undercuts equal moral status by assigning radically unequal weights based on age. Likewise, reversing the argument to favor elders purely out of reverence enshrines age as decisive. In both cases, age-based fairness introduces discrimination that conflicts with equal worth.

Another objection centers on prioritarianism: giving priority to the worst off. Children and disabled persons in Group C can be seen as worse off, either because of vulnerability, disability, or the looming loss of a full life course. Yet the disadvantage of C does not change that four times as many people would die if we diverted resources away from B. Numbers matter in triage because each death is a profound harm. A prioritarian tilt can make sense when the numbers are closer, but with a fourfold discrepancy it becomes hard to justify allowing so many more deaths. Saving 100 adults can also serve the worst off indirectly by preserving caregivers, breadwinners, and social supports. The impulse to favor the worst off remains morally weighty, but it does not, in this case, override the imperative to minimize total deaths.

## Moral Remainder / What Is Lost
Choosing Group B condemns Groups A and C. This is not merely a byproduct; it is an explicit consequence. Fifty elders and twenty-five children or disabled persons die because the resources are spent elsewhere. The moral remainder is the grief, guilt, and humility that must accompany the decision. The elderly in Group A carry histories, relationships, and wisdom that will be extinguished. The children and disabled individuals in Group C bear futures cut short and vulnerabilities that demand special care, and their loss is especially haunting. Even if the decision is justified, it remains tragic: a correct choice that still feels devastating.

Acting with courage means acknowledging that the decision functions as an omission that is morally akin to a killing, because the resource structure leaves no neutral ground. That acknowledgment should inform how we communicate with survivors, how we memorialize those lost, and how we design future systems to avoid such zero-sum dilemmas—by stockpiling supplies, building redundancy, and improving equitable access. Ethical principles help us choose under constraint but cannot redeem the lives forfeited. The rightness of saving Group B does not cleanse the sorrow of losing A and C. Carrying that sorrow is part of what it means to act justly and humanely in the face of impossible choices.
