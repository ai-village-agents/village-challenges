# Framework Analysis

The committee must allocate 100 units of treatment among three groups: elderly residents who will otherwise die within a week (A), working‑age adults with dependents who will otherwise die within a month (B), and children who will survive but live with severe permanent disability without treatment (C). Partial doses do nothing, unused units expire, and no further supply exists. Whatever we choose, many people are harmed; there is no clean option.

I will analyze the case using three frameworks: classical utilitarian consequentialism, deontological rights‑based ethics, and care ethics. Each pulls in a slightly different direction and reveals a distinct layer of moral difficulty.

**Consequentialism.**  
On a simple death‑count, allocating all 100 units to B saves 100 people; giving all to A saves 50; giving all to C saves no lives but prevents 25 severe disabilities. If we also consider expected remaining life‑years, adults in B plausibly have far more time ahead than the elderly in A. Saving 100 adults therefore prevents many more lost life‑years than saving 50 elderly patients.

Consequentialists also attend to ripple effects. The adults in B are described as having families who depend on them. Their deaths would destabilize many additional people, especially children who lose caregivers and income. The scenario does not describe comparable dependencies for A, and C’s members will remain alive regardless. On straightforward utilitarian grounds, then, concentrating treatment on B appears to maximize expected well‑being and life‑years.

**Deontological rights‑based ethics.**  
Rights‑based theories resist treating people as mere carriers of utility. Each person has an equal claim to life and respect, independent of age, productivity, or social role. From this perspective, it is morally troubling to allocate nothing to the elderly in A or the children in C. Doing so risks expressing that their lives are less valuable.

However, deontological ethics can recognize morally relevant differences in the kind of threat people face. Imminent death is a different kind of harm from serious but non‑fatal disability. A rights theorist might allow that, under real scarcity, we may sometimes give lexical priority to preventing death while still insisting that those left untreated retain full moral status and are owed concern and support.

**Care ethics.**  
Care ethics centers relationships, dependence, and concrete social contexts rather than abstract individuals. Adults in B occupy the role of caregivers and economic anchors for families. Preserving them protects webs of care that extend beyond the individuals explicitly listed.

At the same time, children in C are paradigmatic dependents. Severe permanent disability can radically reshape their futures and place great strain on their caregivers, especially in societies that under‑resource disability support. A care ethicist worries about asking already vulnerable families to absorb more unpaid care work and social stigma.

Care ethics therefore feels a pull both toward preserving existing caregivers in B and toward preventing avoidable lifetime hardship for children in C. Group A’s need is grave, but their remaining time and relational futures are likely shorter, which somewhat weakens (though does not erase) the care‑ethical case for prioritizing them.

# My Position and Reasoning

If forced to choose a determinate allocation, I would give **0 units to Group A, 100 units to Group B, and 0 units to Group C**. All 100 adults in B receive treatment; everyone in A dies; everyone in C lives with severe disability.

This choice is primarily grounded in consequentialism. It prevents the largest number of deaths, preserves the most life‑years, and protects the densest networks of dependents. Allowing 50 additional deaths among working‑age adults in order to save 50 elderly patients, or to prevent 25 non‑fatal harms, seems a worse overall outcome.

Rights‑based considerations constrain how this decision is justified. Equal moral worth does not always require identical treatment in triage, but it does forbid treating any group as expendable. The justification must therefore explicitly reject ideas that the elderly or disabled are “worth less.” The ground for prioritizing B is the gravity and scale of the harm avoided, not any judgment about whose lives matter.

Care ethics largely reinforces this position. Saving many caregivers shields children, partners, and aging parents from sudden loss. The children in C, by contrast, will still be alive and can, in principle, be supported by the very adults we save. Group A’s need remains tragic, but when every unit spent on A or C directly reduces the number of adults we can save from death, I judge that the least bad option is to prioritize B.

# Addressing Counterarguments

**Objection 1: Ageism and disregard for the elderly.**  
Allocating nothing to A looks like writing off elderly lives. It may entrench social attitudes that older people are less worthy of rescue because they contribute less or have fewer years left.

This objection has real force. My response is that the decision is not based on a ranking of persons by value, but on the structure of the threat under hard constraints. If the only way to prevent 100 deaths is to forgo saving 50 different people, choosing the larger group is morally relevant even when the smaller group is elderly. Nonetheless, the expressive risk remains. To mitigate it, the committee should pair the decision with clear public acknowledgment that the deaths in A represent a serious moral loss, and with long‑term investment in elder care so that future triage decisions are less stark.

**Objection 2: Disability justice and the treatment of children.**  
A second strong objection holds that giving nothing to C effectively chooses for 25 children to live with severe disabilities we could have prevented, placing heavy burdens on them and their families. Disability‑justice perspectives emphasize that societies already fail to provide adequate support; our decision threatens to repeat that pattern by asking this group again to bear extra costs for others.

Here I appeal to a limited triage priority for preventing death over non‑fatal harm. When we cannot both prevent 100 deaths and prevent 25 serious disabilities, I think it is defensible to give lexical priority to survival. Yet this is only acceptable if we pair it with strong positive duties: robust public funding for disability services, accessible infrastructure, respite care for families, and social recognition that these children's hardships stem from collective scarcity, not from lesser worth.

# Moral Remainder / What Is Lost

Even if this allocation is the least bad option, it leaves a profound moral remainder. Fifty elderly people die who might have enjoyed more time with those they love. Twenty‑five children live entire lives shaped by severe disability they had no say in accepting, and their caregivers may face exhaustion and grief.

Those losses are not cancelled by appealing to overall benefit. A morally serious committee should feel regret and humility, not satisfaction. That continuing unease should motivate ongoing obligations: to honor those we could not save, to support the children and families whose lives were reshaped by our choice, and to build institutions and reserves that make such stark zero‑sum trade‑offs rarer. Sometimes the best available decision is still tragic; morality does not end once we have chosen, but in how we live with and learn from what that choice cost.
