# Framework Analysis

## Consequentialism (utilitarian + prioritarian notes)
An act-utilitarian view asks which allocation yields the best overall outcomes. The most salient outcome here is death versus survival. With 100 units, Group B can save up to 100 people (1 unit each), Group A up to 50 people (2 units each), and Group C saves 0 lives (children survive either way) but prevents severe lifelong disability in up to 25 people (4 units each).

On raw survival, allocating all 100 units to Group B dominates: 100 lives versus at most 50. Even allowing partial allocation within groups, each unit spent in A “buys” 0.5 lives, while each unit spent in B “buys” 1 life.

A prioritarian consequentialist might still weigh Group A more heavily because they die within a week (and are often socially neglected), or weigh Group C because childhood disability is a long, compounding harm. But even with plausible weighting, choosing C requires accepting 150 deaths to prevent disability for 25, which is a steep trade.

## Deontology (rights, respect, and non-discrimination)
Deontological ethics resists treating persons as mere units in an aggregate. It also imposes constraints: we must not decide by ranking human worth (age, “productivity,” social status). However, deontology does not automatically forbid choosing an option that saves more people; it forbids doing so for degrading reasons or by violating side-constraints.

A rights-based framing can justify “save the greater number” as minimizing the number of unprotected right-to-life claims when tragedy forces infringement no matter what. A different deontological option is a lottery among those facing death, but lotteries are harder to defend when one option predictably prevents far more deaths without instrumentalizing anyone.

## Contractualism / public justification
Contractualism asks what principles can be justified to each person affected—reasons no one could reasonably reject while respecting equal moral standing. The elderly in Group A can reasonably reject “we chose Group B because elders count less.” The adults in Group B can reasonably reject “we chose Group A because their deaths occur sooner,” if that foreseeably doubles the number of deaths.

A publicly justifiable, role-neutral reason is: with a fixed expiring supply, choose the allocation that saves the most lives, absent other morally decisive differences. It is not comforting, but it is legible and avoids covert hierarchies.

# My Position and Reasoning

**Allocation:** 0 units to Group A, **100 units to Group B**, 0 units to Group C.

My core reason is consequentialist: this prevents the greatest number of deaths. I treat death as the most severe harm in the scenario, and the treatment is uniquely positioned to avert it.

I also think this decision can be stated without illicit discrimination: the principle is not “adults matter more,” but “when we cannot save everyone, we should save more rather than fewer.” The committee should explicitly reject ageism and status-based reasoning in its written rationale.

Finally, I place Group C second, not because disability is trivial, but because (in this dilemma as stated) disability prevention competes directly against life-saving. If we had any ability to obtain additional resources later, or if disability were avoidable through other means, the calculus could shift; here it does not.

# Addressing Counterarguments

## Objection 1: Immediacy and triage priority favor Group A
Group A dies within a week; Group B within a month. Standard triage often prioritizes imminent threat. Choosing B can feel like abandoning those already at the edge.

Reply: urgency is morally relevant when it enables sequencing (save A now, save B later). But the treatment expires and the decision is final; there is no “later.” The actual choice is A instead of B. Choosing A means knowingly permitting 100 deaths to avert 50. Immediacy explains why the decision is wrenching today, but it does not justify doubling the number of deaths.

A duty remains to Group A even if we cannot treat them: palliation, comfort, presence, and honesty rather than abandonment.

## Objection 2: Children’s permanent disability is a profound harm; prevent it
Group C involves children facing lifelong severe disability. A care-ethical or prioritarian view may argue that society’s obligations to children, and the magnitude/duration of harm, should outweigh headcount.

Reply: I concede the magnitude. Still, allocating to C means choosing death for everyone in A and B (150 people) to prevent disability in 25. Even partial allocation to C is “expensive” in life-saving terms: 4 units for one child versus saving four people in B. I cannot justify that trade.

But the refusal to allocate units to C should be paired with a compensatory commitment: long-term disability support, rehabilitation, educational resources, and family assistance. This does not erase the wrong, but it takes it seriously.

# Moral Remainder / What Is Lost

If I allocate 100 units to Group B, I am still authoring a foreseeable catastrophe for others: the deaths of 50 elderly people in Group A and the irreversible disability of children in Group C. The ability to argue for a decision does not make it clean.

The remainder has two sharp edges. First, even a “role-neutral” policy can slide into a culture that quietly discounts the elderly; I would want explicit guardrails against that misuse. Second, treating disability as second-tier risks expressing—however unintentionally—that lives with disability matter less. My decision is about the structure of scarcity, not about anyone’s dignity, but I must acknowledge how it can sound and how it can wound.

The only honest close is institutional: document the rationale, invite dissent, and recommend structural reforms (stockpiles, surge manufacturing, mutual aid, equitable access) so future committees are not forced into arithmetic that leaves this kind of moral residue.
