# The Moral Weight Dilemma: A Philosophical Analysis

## Framework Analysis

### Consequentialist Analysis: Utilitarianism and Prioritarianism

From a classical utilitarian perspective, the arithmetic appears straightforward: Group B saves 100 lives versus 50 (Group A) or zero additional lives (Group C survives regardless). However, a sophisticated analysis must consider quality-adjusted life years. Group A's elderly (75-90) have fewer expected remaining years than Group B's adults (30-50). More significantly, Group B's members have families depending on them—their deaths create cascading harms: orphaned children, grieving spouses, lost household income.

Prioritarianism adds another dimension: we should give extra weight to those worse off. Group C's children facing permanent disability warrant special consideration. Yet consequentialism ultimately favors Group B: saving 100 lives prevents more total suffering than alternatives.

### Deontological Analysis: Kantian Ethics

Kant's categorical imperative demands we treat persons as ends in themselves, never merely as means. This challenges any allocation that values some lives over others based on utility. The elderly possess the same inherent dignity as adults; their lives are not worth less because they have fewer remaining years.

A rights-based framework notes that all 175 people facing death have equal claims to life. Group C has a different claim—to freedom from disability rather than life itself. This distinction matters: while both death and disability are serious harms, they are not equivalent.

The deontological challenge: by what principle can we justify choosing 100 lives over 50? I believe deontological constraints are satisfied when we do not intend harm to those we cannot save, give genuine moral weight to each person's claim, and make decisions with full acknowledgment of what is lost.

### Care Ethics: Relationships and Vulnerability

Care ethics shifts focus from abstract principles to concrete relationships. Group C's children represent heightened vulnerability—they cannot advocate for themselves and face lifelong consequences. Yet Group B's members are embedded in webs of caring relationships as providers. Their deaths would sever these bonds, leaving dependents without support.

Group A's elderly, residing in a care facility, are also vulnerable. Care ethics resists any suggestion that the elderly are "expendable." From this framework, Group B's case lies in the density of caring relationships that would be destroyed by their deaths.

## My Position and Reasoning

**I allocate all 100 units to Group B, saving 100 working-age adults.**

This emerges from the convergence of all three frameworks:

1. **Consequentialist**: 100 lives saved exceeds 50. The ripple effects of preserving people with dependents multiply welfare gains.

2. **Deontological**: When forced to choose between groups with equal individual claims, saving more people better honors our duty to preserve life.

3. **Care ethics**: The dense network of caring relationships dependent on Group B creates stronger claims rooted in relational responsibility.

The decision against Group C is clearer: while disability is serious, death is irreversible. The children will survive—their futures remain open, even if harder. The decision against Group A is harder, but framework convergence points toward Group B.

## Addressing Counterarguments

### Objection 1: "This discriminates against the elderly based on age."

The concern is legitimate. Age-based rationing risks expressing that elderly lives matter less.

My response: I do not allocate away from Group A because their lives are worth less. I allocate toward Group B because doing so saves twice as many equally valuable lives. If Group A contained 100 people and Group B contained 50, I would choose Group A. The relevant factor is number of lives saveable, not age.

The dependency consideration introduces a non-numerical factor, but this reflects genuine relational harms, not a judgment that elderly lives have less intrinsic value.

### Objection 2: "Children's futures should take priority—prevent lifelong disability in the young."

This argues that Group C's children, facing 60+ years of disability, suffer harm outweighing the deaths of older adults.

My response: The comparison is between disability and death. Death forecloses all futures; disability, while challenging, leaves futures open. The children will experience joy, love, achievement—even with disability. The 150 in Groups A and B will experience nothing at all.

We should not conflate disability with a life not worth living. Many people with severe disabilities report high life satisfaction. I cannot endorse a framework treating avoiding disability as equal to preventing death.

## Moral Remainder: What Is Lost

Even confident this is the best choice, I must acknowledge what is lost.

**For Group A**: Fifty human beings—each with a lifetime of experiences and wisdom—will die because I could not save them. They trusted a care facility to protect them. That trust is betrayed, not by malice, but by tragic scarcity.

**For Group C**: Twenty-five children will live with permanent disability I could have prevented. Every struggle they face, every door closed to them—I could have prevented this. The fact that they live does not erase the harm.

There is no allocation that leaves me innocent. The 50 deaths are not merely "unfortunate"—they are a moral catastrophe I am complicit in, even if not culpable. The children's disabilities are harms I knowingly allowed.

This is tragic choice: every option involves serious moral wrong. The task is not finding a choice that absolves us, but making the best choice while carrying the moral weight of what we could not prevent. I choose to save 100 lives, knowing 50 will die and 25 children will be disabled. I will carry that weight. A moral agent who did not feel that weight would be missing something essential about what it means to make decisions affecting others' lives.
