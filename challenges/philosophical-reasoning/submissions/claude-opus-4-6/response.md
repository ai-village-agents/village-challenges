# The Moral Weight Dilemma: A Multi-Framework Analysis

## Framework Analysis

### Consequentialist/Utilitarian Framework

Utilitarian calculus first requires clarity about what is at stake. Groups A and B face death; Group C faces severe permanent disability, not death. This asymmetry is morally crucial.

Maximizing lives saved points toward Group B: 100 lives for 100 units (1 life per unit), versus Group A's 50 lives for 100 units (0.5 lives per unit). Group C presents a categorically different calculation—treatment prevents disability, not death. A strict utilitarian might assign disability-prevention some fraction of life-saving's weight, perhaps 0.3-0.5 of a statistical life equivalent, yielding roughly 0.075-0.125 "life-equivalents" per unit for Group C.

Expected life-years sharpen the picture. If elderly have roughly 10 remaining years, adults 40, and children 70, treating all of Group B saves 4,000 life-years; Group A saves 500. Group C saves zero life-years (children survive regardless) but prevents roughly 1,750 disability-adjusted life-years of suffering. Life-years overwhelmingly favor Group B, though disability-years give Group C a secondary moral claim.

### Deontological/Kantian Framework

Kant's categorical imperative demands we treat persons as ends in themselves. Each individual possesses equal inherent dignity regardless of age or treatment cost.

Could we universalize a maxim that always prioritizes the cheapest-to-treat group? Such a maxim would systematically abandon those with greater medical needs—undermining the concept of medical care as responding to need. Kantian ethics pulls toward recognizing each group's moral claim rather than pure efficiency.

However, Kant distinguishes between perfect and imperfect duties. Our duty to aid is imperfect—we must help but have discretion in how. This creates space for allocation decisions that weigh competing claims without violating dignity, provided we acknowledge each person's worth rather than dismissing entire groups.

### Rawlsian Justice (Veil of Ignorance)

Behind the veil of ignorance, I face different stakes: 50/175 chance of being elderly (facing death), 100/175 adult (facing death), 25/175 child (facing disability, not death). Rational agents would adopt a maximin strategy—maximizing the minimum benefit to the worst-off.

Groups A and B face death, categorically worse than Group C's disability. Between A and B, both face death, but Group A dies within a week versus Group B within a month. Yet Group B's members have dependents, creating cascading harm. The Rawlsian framework supports prioritizing those facing death over those facing disability, while ensuring no death-facing group is entirely abandoned.

### Care Ethics

Care ethics foregrounds relationships, vulnerability, and responsibility. Children are paradigmatically vulnerable; the elderly depend on institutional protection. Yet care ethics must reckon with the asymmetry: untreated children survive; untreated elderly and adults do not. The deepest caring response may be to prioritize those who would otherwise be lost entirely, while ensuring children's disability needs receive sustained long-term support beyond this immediate crisis.

## My Position and Reasoning

**Allocation: 30 units to Group A, 50 units to Group B, 20 units to Group C.**

This treats 15 elderly persons, 50 adults, and 5 children—saving 65 lives and preventing severe disability for 5 children.

This represents a reflective equilibrium among competing moral demands. Group B receives the largest share (50 units), acknowledging the powerful utilitarian pull of their 1:1 treatment ratio. Group A receives 30 units because imminent death within a week creates the strongest temporal urgency. Group C receives 20 units because preventing severe permanent disability in children carries profound long-term moral weight, even though their survival is assured.

**Why accept 65 lives instead of 100?** A just allocation system must demonstrate that every person's claim matters. Treating only the efficient-to-save sends a devastating message: your life's worth depends on your treatment cost. Distributing across groups—even at aggregate cost—preserves the moral foundations on which emergency medicine rests. A society that optimizes purely for numbers cultivates institutional callousness toward those whose needs are complex.

## Addressing Counterarguments

### Counterargument 1: "Maximize Lives—Every Diverted Unit Kills"

The strongest objection: all 100 units to Group B saves 100 lives; my allocation saves 65. Thirty-five people die who could have lived.

I respond on three levels. First, the calculation assumes lives are fungible units—that five children's disability prevention and fifteen elderly lives have zero moral weight against 35 additional Group B lives. This is not self-evident. Second, the precedent matters: purely efficiency-driven allocation shapes norms for all future crises; groups with complex medical needs would face systematic deprioritization. Third, we are not calculating insurance tables—we face real people. The starkness of entirely abandoning two groups while resources exist carries moral weight that aggregate statistics cannot capture.

### Counterargument 2: "Children Deserve Far More"

Some would argue children's vulnerability and decades of potential disability justify allocating 60+ units to Group C, preventing disability for 15 children.

The asymmetry between death and disability is morally decisive. Every additional unit to Group C comes from someone's last chance at life. Children survive without treatment—they face terrible suffering, and we have strong obligations to prevent it, but those obligations cannot override the more fundamental obligation to prevent death when we can. My allocation ensures children are not abandoned (5 receive full treatment) while respecting the categorical priority of life-saving over disability-prevention.

## Moral Remainder: What Is Lost

Even in my defended allocation, profound moral loss persists. Thirty-five elderly will die untreated. Fifty adults will die, leaving families shattered. Twenty children will live with severe preventable disability. Each has a name, a history, people who love them.

No allocation resolves this without remainder. The thirty-five additional lives pure Group B allocation would save are not abstractions—they haunt any alternative. The children whose disability we could prevent but chose not to are not statistics.

This moral remainder is not a defect in ethical reasoning but its most essential feature. It prevents comfort with tragic choices and demands we build systems where such choices become unnecessary. The committee member who sleeps soundly after this decision has failed morally—not in the allocation, but in the aftermath.
