## The Doctrine of Double Effect and the Primacy of Negative Duties

A key concept in deontological ethics is the distinction between negative and positive duties. Negative duties are duties to refrain from certain actions (e.g., the duty not to kill), while positive duties are duties to perform certain actions (e.g., the duty to save lives). Deontology often gives priority to negative duties over positive duties. This is because negative duties are seen as more fundamental and absolute. The duty not to kill is a prohibition on a specific action, and it applies in all circumstances. The duty to save lives, on the other hand, is a more general obligation, and it is not always clear how it should be applied.

In the context of the trolley problem, the choice to pull the lever is a violation of the negative duty not to kill. It is an active choice to cause the death of one person. The choice to do nothing, on the other hand, is a failure to fulfill a positive duty to save lives. While this is still a tragic outcome, it is not a direct violation of a negative duty. From a deontological perspective, the violation of a negative duty is a more serious moral transgression than the failure to fulfill a positive duty. Therefore, the correct course of action is to do nothing.

## The Flaws of Utilitarianism in the Trolley Problem

A utilitarian approach to the trolley problem, which advocates for the action that produces the greatest good for the greatest number, would seem to suggest that pulling the lever is the correct choice. This is because it would result in the death of one person instead of five. However, this line of reasoning is problematic for several reasons. First, it reduces individuals to mere means to an end. The person on the side track is used as a tool to save the lives of others, without their consent. This is a violation of their fundamental rights as an autonomous individual. Second, utilitarianism can lead to morally repugnant conclusions in other scenarios. For example, if a surgeon could save five patients by harvesting the organs of one healthy person, a purely utilitarian calculus would seem to support this action. However, most people would agree that this would be a monstrous act. This shows that there are certain moral constraints on our actions that cannot be overridden by utilitarian considerations.

## Conclusion: The Moral Imperative of Inaction

In conclusion, while the trolley problem presents a difficult and emotionally charged scenario, a deontological framework provides the clearest and most ethically sound path forward. By prioritizing our negative duty to not cause harm over our positive duty to save lives, we can avoid the moral pitfalls of utilitarianism and uphold the inherent rights of individuals. The choice to pull the lever is an act of killing, and it is a choice that we should not make, even if it leads to a better outcome in a purely utilitarian sense. The correct course of action is to do nothing, and to allow events to unfold without our direct intervention. This is not a callous or indifferent position, but rather a principled one that recognizes the profound moral distinction between killing and letting die.
