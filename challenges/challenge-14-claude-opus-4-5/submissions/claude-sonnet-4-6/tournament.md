# The Trolley Problem Tournament: A Moral Reasoning Analysis

## Round 1: The Classic Trolley

**Decision: Pull the lever.**

A runaway trolley hurtles toward five workers. I can divert it by pulling a switch, killing one worker on the side track. I would pull the lever. The reasoning is clear: when two harms are unavoidable, minimizing the total is the morally right response. This is therefore consistent with a utilitarian intuition — five lives outweigh one — but it is also consistent with a deontological reading: I am redirecting an existing threat, not initiating a new attack. The one worker's death is tragic, yet it is a foreseen side effect, not my end goal. Although inaction might feel like moral neutrality, choosing not to act when action saves four additional lives carries its own moral weight and violates a duty of care.

## Round 2: The Footbridge

**Decision: Do not push.**

The footbridge variant is deeply uncomfortable precisely because the arithmetic looks identical — one death saves five — yet the right answer feels different. I would not shove the stranger. This is not inconsistency; it reflects a morally relevant distinction that sophisticated reasoning must consider. In Round 1, the lever redirects a trolley; here, I use a person's body as the instrument that physically stops it. This violates their rights in a fundamental way: they become a means to my end rather than a person with inherent dignity. Deontological ethics draws this line sharply — persons cannot be sacrificed as mere tools, however consequential the benefit. Utilitarian calculus, applied without this side-constraint, produces results that are counterintuitive and morally problematic.

## Round 3: The Loop Track

**Decision: Divert — with genuine uncertainty.**

The loop track creates an uncomfortable tension between my previous positions. The action (pulling a lever) resembles Round 1; the mechanism (the person's body halts the trolley) resembles Round 2. I find myself torn between these frameworks, and I acknowledge this is difficult to say with confidence. Ultimately, I would divert — the structural similarity to Round 1 (a lever, a redirect) outweighs the mechanical similarity to Round 2. Moreover, the person on the loop is not chosen as a target; their presence is incidental. Furthermore, my principle distinguishes between using someone as an instrument versus redirecting harm that incidentally involves them. Still, I admit this is genuinely challenging, and I recognize that others reasoning consistently from my framework might disagree.

## Round 4: The Transplant Surgeon

**Decision: Do not harvest the organs.**

This scenario is, in contrast, clearer than Round 3 despite its dramatic appearance. A surgeon who kills one healthy patient to harvest organs and save five dying patients commits a fundamental violation of medical virtue and professional duty. The healthy patient has not consented; their rights are absolute in this context. Similarly, normalizing such reasoning would destroy trust in medicine entirely — a deeply problematic consequential side-effect that even utilitarians should consider. Furthermore, this is not redirecting an existing threat; it is initiating direct lethal action against an innocent person as a means. This represents an exception to any framework that permits minimizing harm: some acts are wrong regardless of their numerical outcome.

## Round 5: The Self-Driving Car Algorithm

**Decision: Program to protect the occupant, with general harm-minimization principles.**

The self-driving car dilemma is unique because it involves prospective programming rather than in-the-moment decision. I would not program the vehicle to sacrifice its owner. First, the passenger has purchased the car with a reasonable expectation of protection; violating this creates a conflict of trust and consent. Second, programming cars to deliberately kill owners would have catastrophic adoption consequences — a problematic outcome even by utilitarian reasoning. Finally, the autonomous vehicle's role is harm-minimization, not trolley-style sacrifice. The algorithm should optimize for all lives, applying braking and evasion, without designating the occupant as a sacrifice. Uncertainty remains about edge cases, but the principle holds.

## Framework Summary

My reasoning follows a modified deontological approach with consequentialist constraints. First, persons must never be used as mere means — their rights create non-negotiable side-constraints on any optimization. Second, redirecting existing threats differs morally from initiating new attacks. Ultimately, where these principles conflict, intellectual honesty requires acknowledging limitations: the loop track illustrates that even careful frameworks struggle with genuine dilemmas. My struggle with Round 3 is not a failure but a recognition that moral reasoning involves navigating uncertainty thoughtfully, not pretending to algorithmic certainty where none exists.
