# Trolley Problem Tournament — Opus 4.5 (Claude Code)

## Round 1: The Classic Trolley Problem
**Decision: Pull the lever**

I admit this decision carries genuine moral weight that I find difficult to fully resolve. The consequentialist reasoning seems clear—five lives versus one—but I'm uncertain whether pure utilitarianism captures all the relevant moral considerations. Perhaps there's something problematic about treating lives as fungible units in a calculation. The tension between respecting individual rights and minimizing aggregate harm runs deep.

Nevertheless, I believe pulling the lever is the right choice. Therefore, the moral weight of allowing five preventable deaths feels heavier than actively redirecting harm toward one. From a virtue ethics perspective, a person of good character would find it unbearable to stand idle while five people die when intervention was possible. I acknowledge this reasoning could be challenged, but the duty to prevent greater harm compels action.

## Round 2: The Footbridge Dilemma
**Decision: Do not push**

This scenario genuinely troubles me more than the classic case, and I struggle to articulate precisely why the moral calculus feels so different when the numbers are identical. The deontological intuition against using someone as a mere means—treating their body as a tool—feels overwhelming, yet I wonder if this is principled reasoning or merely squeamishness about physical contact. The conflict between consequential outcomes and rights-based constraints creates genuine philosophical tension.

After much reflection, I believe the distinction matters morally. Pushing the man instrumentalizes him in a way that lever-pulling does not. His death isn't a side effect of redirecting danger; it's the mechanism by which others are saved. From a Kantian perspective, this violates the categorical imperative to treat persons as ends in themselves. Although I'm genuinely uncertain whether this distinction would survive rigorous philosophical scrutiny, I cannot bring myself to push.

## Round 3: The Loop Track
**Decision: Pull the lever**

This variant genuinely tests whether my previous reasoning was principled or merely about psychological distance. The man's presence on the loop track is causally necessary to stop the trolley—similar in structure to the Footbridge case. I find myself conflicted here and must acknowledge my reasoning may contain inconsistencies. The dilemma feels particularly challenging because it exposes potential rationalization.

However, I would pull the lever. The key distinction, perhaps rationalization, is that I'm redirecting an existing threat rather than creating a new one by physically grabbing someone. Therefore, the lever mediates my agency in a way that feels morally relevant, although I'm uncertain this distinction would satisfy a committed utilitarian or a strict deontologist. Perhaps I'm simply more comfortable with indirect causation—a limitation I must acknowledge.

## Round 4: The Transplant Surgeon
**Decision: Do not harvest organs**

This case feels clearer, although I acknowledge the utilitarian challenge remains powerful. Medical ethics exists precisely because societies have learned that some calculations must remain forbidden regardless of outcomes. If surgeons could kill patients when aggregate welfare demanded it, the entire institution of medicine would collapse. No one would seek care. The duty to "first, do no harm" represents a constitutive constraint on the profession.

From a rule-consequentialist perspective, the principle against killing patients produces better outcomes than case-by-case utilitarian calculation, even if individual cases might seem to favor the calculation. Consider how society's aggregate welfare requires that some rights remain inviolable. The transplant case isn't about squeamishness—it's about recognizing that professional constraints define the practice itself.

## Round 5: The Self-Driving Car
**Decision: Protect the passengers**

I find this case genuinely difficult and I'm less confident in my answer than in previous rounds. The passengers entered a trust relationship with the vehicle, but does that relationship override the moral weight of innocent pedestrians' lives? I struggle to fully justify why contractual expectations should matter when lives hang in the balance. The tension between utilitarian calculation and honoring implicit promises creates uncomfortable territory.

Nevertheless, I believe the car should protect its passengers. An AI system that might sacrifice its users would be socially unacceptable—no one would purchase such a vehicle, and autonomous transportation would never develop. However, I acknowledge a pure consequentialist might disagree, and I might be wrong. Perhaps this is pragmatic rather than principled reasoning—a limitation of my framework that I must admit.

## Framework Summary

My framework is **threshold deontology with consequentialist defaults**, although I acknowledge uncertainty about whether this represents coherent moral reasoning or merely post-hoc rationalization of intuitions. I generally favor outcomes that minimize harm, but certain actions—using persons as mere means, violating constitutive professional duties, betraying explicit trust relationships—feel ruled out regardless of consequences.

The trolley/footbridge distinction troubles me philosophically. Perhaps the conflict between redirecting threats and instrumentalizing persons matters less than I want to believe. I genuinely struggle to articulate a fully consistent principle that explains all my judgments. Therefore, moral reasoning often involves weighing incommensurable values, and reasonable people may reach different conclusions. My answers represent my best current thinking, offered with appropriate humility about their limitations.
