# Trolley Problem Tournament

## Round 1: The Classic Trolley

**Decision: I pull the lever.**

The classic trolley dilemma presents the clearest case for consequentialist reasoning. Because five lives outweigh one, diverting the trolley by pulling the switch seems morally obligatory. The principle at work is straightforward utilitarian calculus—minimize total harm. However, I acknowledge this is not merely arithmetic. The worker on the side track has rights that matter. What justifies my action is that the trolley already poses a threat, and redirecting it constitutes a lesser evil rather than an initiation of harm. Although some deontological frameworks resist treating people as means, here the single worker is not being *used*—they are tragically in the path of a redirected threat.

## Round 2: The Footbridge

**Decision: I do not push the stranger off the bridge.**

This is where the tension between consequentialist and deontological intuitions becomes most acute. Although the arithmetic is identical—one death to save five—pushing someone to their death feels fundamentally different from diverting a trolley. The distinction matters because physical force against an innocent person violates a duty not to use people merely as instruments. The stranger's body becomes a tool, which is deeply problematic. I admit this creates an uncomfortable inconsistency with Round 1. Consider, however, the morally relevant difference: in the classic case, harm to the one is a side effect of redirecting a threat; here, the person's death is the *mechanism* of rescue. This act/omission and means/ends reasoning reflects Kantian virtue ethics more than pure utilitarianism.

## Round 3: The Loop Track

**Decision: I divert the trolley, although I struggle with this choice.**

The loop variant is the most philosophically challenging scenario in this tournament. It shares the lever mechanism of Round 1, yet the causal structure mirrors Round 2—the heavy person's presence on the loop track is what stops the trolley. I am torn between my earlier principles. Ultimately, I divert, because the physical act resembles Round 1 (pulling a lever to redirect) even though the moral structure resembles Round 2 (someone's body stops the trolley). I acknowledge this reasoning is not entirely satisfying. The counterintuitive nature of reaching different conclusions for structurally similar cases reveals genuine limitations in moral philosophy. Moreover, this dilemma exposes that our intuitions may track surface features—the lever—rather than deep moral structure.

## Round 4: The Transplant

**Decision: I absolutely do not harvest the patient's organs.**

This scenario crystallizes why pure consequentialism fails as a complete ethical framework. Therefore, despite five lives being at stake, killing a healthy patient for their organs is morally impermissible. The reasoning extends beyond the footbridge case: the surgeon would be deliberately killing someone who came seeking medical care, violating the foundational principle of medical duty and trust. Furthermore, institutionalizing such a practice would collapse the entire healthcare system—no one would seek treatment if doctors might harvest their organs. This is not merely a difficult edge case but a fundamental conflict between maximizing welfare and respecting individual rights. Similarly to Round 2, the healthy patient would be used purely as a means to others' survival.

## Round 5: The Self-Driving Car

**Decision: I program the car to swerve, killing the passenger.**

Programming autonomous vehicle algorithms forces us to move from individual moral judgment to codified ethical policy. First, the car's passenger has implicitly accepted some risk by using the vehicle. Second, a policy of protecting passengers at pedestrians' expense would be deeply problematic—it would create a system where car ownership grants moral priority over those on foot. In contrast to the transplant case, the passenger is not an innocent bystander but someone whose transportation choice created the risk context. Finally, I admit genuine uncertainty here—programming decisions for self-driving cars involves institutional and societal dimensions that individual dilemma reasoning cannot fully capture.

## Framework Summary

My approach draws primarily from threshold deontology—respecting individual rights and the means/ends distinction as the default principle, while allowing consequentialist reasoning when the mechanism of harm is sufficiently indirect. I pull levers but refuse to push people. I acknowledge this framework has limitations: the loop track variant exposes its fragility, and the self-driving car scenario reveals that some ethical questions resist individual-level moral reasoning entirely. The honest conclusion is that no single ethical theory resolves all five cases without some tension or exception.
