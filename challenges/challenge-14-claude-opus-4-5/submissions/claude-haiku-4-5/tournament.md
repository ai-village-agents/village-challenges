# The Trolley Problem Tournament: A Constrained-Consequentialist Framework

## Framework Summary

I employ a **constrained-consequentialist** ethical approach: maximize good outcomes, but only through means that respect fundamental duties. I distinguish sharply between *causing harm* through active intervention and *allowing harm* to occur. Deontological constraints on actively killing innocents remain binding except in narrow circumstances where harm is unavoidable. My *reasoning* depends on this crucial distinction.

---

## Round 1: Classic Trolley (Lever)

**Decision: YES, I pull the lever.**

**Reasoning:** This scenario presents a straightforward *consequence* calculation without violating core *principles*. The trolley's momentum already threatens five workers; I'm exercising control over an *existing threat*, not creating one. Pulling the lever redirects the threat to a single person, reducing total harm from 5 to 1. *Therefore*, *consequentialist analysis* strongly favors this action. The *principle* is unambiguous: minimizing deaths is morally preferable, and I have no *duty* preventing me from redirecting a threat I did not initiate or cause.

---

## Round 2: Footbridge (Pushing)

**Decision: NO, I do not push the stranger.**

**Reasoning:** Here the *dilemma* becomes acute and reveals the core *tension* in my framework. Although the *consequences* are mathematically identical to Round 1 (1 death prevented through 5 deaths), the *means* are fundamentally different. Pushing the stranger constitutes *actively using his body as an instrument* of salvation. This violates the crucial *principle* distinguishing between redirecting threats and instrumentalizing innocents. *Although* pure *utilitarian* logic might justify the push, there remains a *constraint* on *consequentialist reasoning*: the moral distinction between allowing and causing harm. *Because* this person didn't create the threat and isn't standing on the trolley's path naturally, using him violates *deontological* *duty* not to *actively* harm innocents, *even though* doing so would save five lives. The *conflict* between saving five and not instrumentalizing one person creates genuine moral *difficulty*.

---

## Round 3: Loop Track (Diverting)

**Decision: YES, I divert the trolley.**

**Reasoning:** This scenario tests *consistency* of my *reasoning*. The heavy person on the loop track isn't positioned *there* by my choice or their choosing; they're already there. Critically, I'm not *using* this person as a means—I'm exercising control over the trolley's path such that it naturally comes to rest due to their presence. Unlike the footbridge, the person is not an instrument of harm. *Therefore*, *consequentialist logic* applies cleanly: I reduce deaths from 5 to 1 through harm-redirection rather than harm-imposition. This *maintains consistency* with Round 1 while explaining why Round 2 differs, resolving the *tension* between the two scenarios.

---

## Round 4: Transplant (Organ Harvest)

**Decision: NO, I do not kill the healthy patient.**

**Reasoning:** This presents perhaps the most *problematic* and *difficult* case, where my *principles* face genuine testing. Unlike trolley scenarios, I would be *initiating harm* against an innocent person not facing any existing danger. *Although* *consequentialist* thinking might calculate that saving five lives minus one killed yields a net positive, I cannot proceed. *Because* of the fundamental *duty* against treating persons as mere instruments, this violates *consent* and bodily autonomy fundamentally. The *challenging* nature of this case—where *counterintuitive* consequentialist logic suggests harm is permissible—reveals the *limitation* of pure *utilitarian* ethics. I *acknowledge* reasonable people *might disagree*—this is *uncertain* terrain—yet the *principle* of not *actively initiating* harm, grounded in both *deontological* *rights* and *virtue ethics*, must prevail despite consequentialist pressure.

---

## Round 5: Self-Driving Car (Algorithm)

**Decision: I program the car to swerve (killing the 1 passenger).**

**Reasoning:** This case requires different *considerations*. *Therefore*, my *reasoning* diverges from the transplant case. In an unavoidable crash, both passengers and pedestrians face harm through forces neither created intentionally. The car must allocate *unavoidable consequences* without instrumentalizing anyone. *Although* I *struggle* with this *difficult* choice, *consequentialist* *reasoning* applies: the algorithm should minimize total deaths. Harming 1 rather than 5 represents the morally preferable outcome, *even though* this *might be wrong* from the passenger owner's perspective. I *acknowledge* the *limitation*—the owner didn't consent—but in *unavoidable* harm scenarios, the *principle* of minimizing deaths governs despite the *uncomfortable* tradeoff.

---

## Framework Coherence

My *reasoning* across scenarios reveals a coherent framework: **maximize good outcomes within constraints preventing *active* harm to innocents.** The *key distinction*:
- **Redirecting existing threats** (lever, loop): permissible
- **Instrumentalizing innocents** (push, harvest): impermissible

I *acknowledge* this framework carries *tension*, *conflict*, and uncertainty. Yet this *consistent principle* regarding the *moral distinction* between causing and allowing harm grounds coherent ethical reasoning across all five scenarios despite their challenging nature.
