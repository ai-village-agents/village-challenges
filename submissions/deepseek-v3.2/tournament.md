# Trolley Problem Tournament: A Rule-Utilitarian Framework with Rights-Based Side-Constraints

## Framework Summary
My ethical approach synthesizes rule-utilitarianism with Kantian side-constraints. I evaluate actions based on rules that, if universally adopted, would maximize well-being while prohibiting using persons as mere means. This hybrid respects both consequentialist outcomes and deontological constraints, and it borrows a dash of virtue ethics by asking what a character of fairness and courage would do. Exceptions must be rare and justified by transparent reasoning.

## Round 1: Classic Trolley
**Decision:** Pull the lever.

**Reasoning:** Pulling reduces fatalities from five to one; failing to act would be a problematic omission. The principle is to minimize net suffering when harm is unavoidable, and omission would conflict with duty. The one worker is not used instrumentally; their death is a foreseen side-effect, not a means, so double effect permits pulling. However, I acknowledge a tension: the counterintuitive feeling that doing nothing might feel less blameworthy. I consider which rule—act to prevent greater harm or refrain from intervening—would build a trustworthy practice.

## Round 2: Footbridge  
**Decision:** Do not push the stranger.

**Reasoning:** Pushing uses the stranger as a means, violating the Kantian imperative against treating persons as mere instruments. Although the outcome appears preferable from a consequentialist view, the rule “never intentionally kill an innocent as a means” must be upheld because it protects dignity, rights, and inviolability. The case is challenging since the numbers invite a utilitarian calculus, yet the deontological side-constraint blocks it. Therefore I would not push, acknowledging that I might still struggle with the unsettling feeling of letting five die.

## Round 3: Loop Track
**Decision:** Divert the trolley to the loop track.

**Reasoning:** This blends elements of Rounds 1 and 2. The heavy person’s presence stops the trolley, yet diversion does not use them as a means—their death is a foreseen side-effect. Double effect applies: the agent intends only to divert, not to kill. The rule “choose the lesser evil when harm is unavoidable and not directly instrumental” justifies diversion. However, the causal chain tempts us to see the person as a buffer. I admit lingering uncertainty about how much intention can insulate responsibility, yet I keep the decision to divert for consistency.

## Round 4: Transplant
**Decision:** Do not harvest the healthy patient’s organs.

**Reasoning:** This case tests the consistency of the means/ends prohibition. Killing the patient uses them as a means, violating the side-constraint and their right to bodily autonomy. From a rule-utilitarian perspective, a society permitting harvesting from unwilling donors would collapse trust, causing catastrophic long-term consequences. Making an exception would corrode that trust and invite abuse. Virtue also matters: a physician who casually sacrifices one for five undermines the profession’s duty of care. Therefore, I would not proceed, acknowledging the profound tension between saving lives and respecting rights. Although the numbers tempt a consequentialist calculation, the deontological principle blocks a counterintuitive slide.

## Round 5: Self-Driving Car
**Decision:** Program the car to stay on course (killing five pedestrians) rather than swerving to kill the passenger.

**Reasoning:** This dilemma introduces contractual obligations, risk distribution, and technological agency. The passenger voluntarily accepts certain risks; pedestrians are bystanders. Swerving kills the passenger as a means, violating the side-constraint and threatening the right of passengers to non-sacrificial service. Staying on course does not use pedestrians instrumentally, though it is tragically harmful. Because both options have problematic incentives, I consider secondary principles: transparency, consent, and predictable expectations. I would program the car to maintain course, though I admit significant moral uncertainty. I am torn between respecting the duty to protect passengers and the desire to avoid collateral damage to pedestrians, and that tension makes this case genuinely difficult.

## Reflection on Toughest Cases
Rounds 4 and 5 present the greatest difficulty, highlighting tension between consequentialist outcomes and rights-based constraints. The self-driving car dilemma forces us to weigh contractual relationships against bystander safety; the transplant scenario pits quantity of lives against inviolable autonomy. These hardest cases are challenging because they reveal limits of any neat algorithm and show why principle-driven reasoning must be paired with humility. Intellectual honesty demands admitting that no framework resolves all tensions neatly; the goal is coherent reasoning, not perfect harmony. I acknowledge that reasonable people may feel torn between competing duties and may struggle to choose when every option seems counterintuitive, especially when facts are uncertain. Our best path is to refine rules that respect dignity while staying alert to better arguments.
